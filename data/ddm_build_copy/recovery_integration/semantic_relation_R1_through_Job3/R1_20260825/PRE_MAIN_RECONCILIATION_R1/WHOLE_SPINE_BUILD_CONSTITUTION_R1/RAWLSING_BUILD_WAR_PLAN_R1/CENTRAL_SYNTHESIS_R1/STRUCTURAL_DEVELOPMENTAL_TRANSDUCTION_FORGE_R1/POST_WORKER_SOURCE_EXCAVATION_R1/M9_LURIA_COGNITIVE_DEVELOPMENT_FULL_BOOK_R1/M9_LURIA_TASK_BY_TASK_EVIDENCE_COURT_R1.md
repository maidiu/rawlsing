# M9 — LURIA TASK-BY-TASK EVIDENCE COURT R1

STATUS: `TASK_FAMILIES_SEPARATED__NO_TOTAL_PSYCHOLOGY_GENERALIZATION`

## Court rule

No result in one task family licenses a claim about a total psychological transformation. Every result remains attached to:

```text
TASK
INSTRUCTION
PROMPT HISTORY
RESPONSE FORMAT
ACTOR/GROUP EXPOSURE BUNDLE
TEST SETTING
```

## T1 — Color naming

TASK: name colored skeins / hues.

OBSERVED: ichkari women use many more figurative/object-linked names than school/collective groups. Table 1: 59.5% figurative names in 11 ichkari women; 30.5% in 15 preschool-course women; 16.7% in 16 collective-farm activists; 16.3% in 10 teachers'-school women.

LAWFUL INFERENCE: naming conventions and semantic organization differ in this task.

CEILING: not proof of different sensory color capacity.

ALTERNATIVES: embroidery expertise, lexical repertoire, task expectations, schooling, age/sex composition, local object ecology.

## T2 — Color classification

TASK: group hues, including forced-number grouping.

OBSERVED: ichkari participants frequently reject grouping unlike hues or arrange continuous brightness/saturation series. Persistent suggestion sometimes produces experimenter-desired categories. Table 2 reports 18.2% failure, 27.3% series arrangement, 54.5% primary-color classification for n=11 ichkari women versus 93.7–100% primary-color classifications in the other three groups.

LAWFUL INFERENCE: experimental grouping convention is not shared equally.

CRITICAL NEGATIVE: `REFUSAL TO GROUP != INABILITY TO DISCRIMINATE OR CATEGORIZE`.

## T3 — Geometrical naming and grouping

TASK: name and classify circles, triangles, quadrangles, incomplete figures, and figures made of subelements.

OBSERVED: object names dominate in less-schooled groups; geometric categorical names dominate in teachers'-school participants. Table 4: categorical-name share 0%, 14.7%, 41%, 84.8% across the four reported groups.

LAWFUL INFERENCE: learned geometric label/category practice strongly tracks task response.

CEILING: Luria's claim that basic laws of shape perception are differently organized goes beyond what naming/classification alone identifies.

## T4 — Optical illusions

TASK: standard line/shape illusions including Müller-Lyer and perspective/structural figures.

OBSERVED: frequency varies by group and illusion; Luria reports increases with education up to 75.6%; Müller-Lyer is widespread even among ichkari women.

SOURCE SELF-CEILING: Luria calls these data preliminary and says dedicated experiments are needed to reveal mechanisms.

RAWLSING: preserve as task-specific perceptual-performance evidence only.

## T5 — Four-object classification

TASK: four drawings; three can share a taxonomic class while practical scenarios can include all four. Instructions use "similar," "one group," "one word"; examples and counterarguments are supplied.

OBSERVED: many illiterate remote peasants construct practical situations and resist excluding an item because all are functionally needed. Some know the generic class word while rejecting taxonomic grouping as pragmatically wrong. Table 7: 80% graphic/situational in n=26 remote peasants; 70% categorical in n=10 barely literate activists; 100% categorical in n=12 young participants with 1–2 years schooling.

DIRECT LESSON: category knowledge, task-goal acceptance, and response strategy are separable.

FORBIDDEN: `SITUATIONAL GROUPING -> ABSENCE OF ABSTRACTION`.

## T6 — Similarity / comparison

TASK: state what two objects share or how they are alike.

OBSERVED: unschooled participants often lead with differences, interactions, or concrete uses; with reframing some provide shared class properties.

ALTERNATIVES: semantics of "similar," relevance norms, school genre familiarity, interviewer demand.

CEILING: not a general measure of abstract capacity.

## T7 — Concept definition

TASK: define familiar objects and social concepts to a hypothetical person who lacks them.

OBSERVED: unschooled participants often reject the need to define obvious objects or give concrete physical/functional descriptions; collective/school participants more often use comparisons and category relations. Social concepts can elicit more generalized definitions than mundane objects.

SIGNIFICANCE: domain/content matters; one cannot assign a single abstraction level to the person.

## T8 — Generic-term meaning

TASK: probe terms such as "tools" and other generic labels.

OBSERVED: some participants use generic terms with practical/functional extensions unlike the experimenter's intended taxonomic boundary.

LAWFUL INFERENCE: lexical category extension is activity/context sensitive.

CEILING: Luria himself calls for further psycholinguistic investigation.

## T9 — Syllogism repetition

TASK: repeat premises before inference.

OBSERVED: some remote unschooled participants transform universals into concrete/particular propositions or questions; schooling-exposed participants more often reproduce the formal schema.

ALTERNATIVES: memory, quantifier semantics, unfamiliar speech genre, translation, conversational pragmatics.

## T10 — Syllogistic inference, familiar content

OBSERVED: 9/15 illiterate remote peasants immediately solved experience-associated syllogisms in Table 8; practical reasoning is repeatedly competent in transcripts.

CEILING: no logical-incapacity inference.

## T11 — Syllogistic inference, unfamiliar content

OBSERVED: only 2/15 illiterate remote peasants immediately solved non-experience-associated syllogisms; 15/15 young short-education/farm-activist participants solved them. Many refusals explicitly invoke lack of firsthand knowledge. Conditional reframing converts additional answers.

KEY COURT:

```text
"I HAVE NOT SEEN / I CANNOT SAY" 
!=
"I CANNOT INFER"
```

The response can instantiate an epistemic-warrant rule.

## T12 — Simple practical word problems

OBSERVED: illiterate remote peasants often calculate successfully; Table 9 gives 12/16 immediate simple-problem solutions and 16/16 after conditions are made more specific.

This directly undermines a blanket arithmetic/reasoning deficit interpretation.

## T13 — Conflict / stipulated word problems

TASK: solve problems whose stated conditions contradict known real distances/facts.

OBSERVED: n=16 remote peasants: 3/16 immediate solutions; 4/16 after increased specificity. Young short-education group n=7: 7/7 immediate. Participants often call the premise false rather than accept it as a hypothetical closed system.

ALTERNATIVES: school genre, counterfactual convention, epistemic authority, real-world knowledge, prompt interpretation.

## T14 — Manipulatives / assistance in problem solving

OBSERVED: concrete aids and explicit arithmetic framing sometimes improve performance but do not uniformly transfer to the target problem.

LAWFUL INFERENCE: assistance uptake is actor/task specific.

FORBIDDEN: one assisted success does not establish durable developmental transformation.

## T15 — Question formulation / "imagination"

TASK: formulate three questions; imaginary situations offered if needed.

OBSERVED: Table 10 shows a steep group gradient from refusal/practical-scenario questions to decontextualized knowledge questions. Some illiterate participants explain that questions require knowledge and show clear curiosity about direct practical matters.

CEILING: this measures question-production under a particular interview convention. It does not measure total imaginative capacity.

SUPPORTED ASSISTANCE EFFECT: an imaginary communicative scenario enables some participants to formulate questions they did not initially produce.

## T16 — Self-description / shortcomings

TASK: describe character, positive qualities, shortcomings, differences from others.

OBSERVED: many remote participants treat "shortcoming" as material lack or report concrete behavior; collective/educated groups more often lexicalize psychological traits. Table 11 shows 5% psychological-feature analysis in n=20 remote participants, 14% in n=15 collective/short-program participants, and 65% in n=17 short-education/activist participants.

ALTERNATIVES: lexical meaning, interview genre, political discourse, social desirability, mutual-evaluation experience, selection.

FORBIDDEN: `MATERIAL/BEHAVIORAL SELF-DESCRIPTION -> ABSENCE OF SELF-AWARENESS`.

## T17 — Other-person evaluation and social feedback

OBSERVED: participants often can describe other people's behavior/qualities even where self-trait language is limited; some explicitly use others' judgments of themselves after participation in meetings/collective evaluation.

SIGNIFICANCE: the content of social feedback is a plausible route into the observed discourse without requiring a group-level psychological prior.

## T18 — Cross-task "syndrome"

Luria treats converging task-family differences as a coherent sociohistorical restructuring of cognition.

COURT: convergence is real at the level of response pattern, but all major tasks share overlapping demands:

- decontextualized verbal instruction;
- accepting experimenter-defined categories;
- treating hypothetical premises as temporarily authoritative;
- producing school-like meta-language;
- interacting with unfamiliar investigators.

Therefore cross-task convergence can also be generated by a common **school/discourse/test-familiarity route**. The design cannot identify a global mental system as the unique latent cause.
