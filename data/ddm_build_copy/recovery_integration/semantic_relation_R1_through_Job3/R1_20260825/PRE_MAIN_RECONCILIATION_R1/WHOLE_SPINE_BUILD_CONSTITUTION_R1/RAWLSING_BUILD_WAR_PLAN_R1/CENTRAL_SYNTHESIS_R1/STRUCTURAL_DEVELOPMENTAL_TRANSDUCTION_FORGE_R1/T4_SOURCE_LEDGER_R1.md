## 1. Core inspected sources

### S1 — Kohn & Schooler 1978

- **Source:** Melvin L. Kohn and Carmi Schooler, “The Reciprocal Effects of the Substantive Complexity of Work and Intellectual Flexibility: A Longitudinal Assessment,” *American Journal of Sociology* 84(1), 1978, pp. 24–52. DOI `10.1086/226739`; JSTOR `2777977`.
- **Inspection:** publisher abstract/metadata plus stable full-text excerpts.
- **Claim status:** longitudinal observational panel; confirmatory factor analysis plus structural-equation causal modeling.
- **Measurement sequence:** 10-year span.
- **Retained result:** substantive complexity of work predicted later intellectual flexibility despite high stability; intellectual flexibility reciprocally predicted later substantive complexity, with the person→job effect described as lagged and gradual.
- **Identification ceiling:** stronger than cross-sectional association, but no randomized job assignment; retain as reciprocal longitudinal evidence, not experimental identification.

### S2 — Kohn & Schooler 1982

- **Source:** Melvin L. Kohn and Carmi Schooler, “Job Conditions and Personality: A Longitudinal Assessment of Their Reciprocal Effects,” *American Journal of Sociology* 87(6), 1982, pp. 1257–1286. DOI `10.1086/227593`; JSTOR `2779361`.
- **Inspection:** publisher record plus page-specific full-text excerpts, especially pp. 1258, 1261, 1281–1282.
- **Claim status:** longitudinal observational panel; confirmatory measurement models and linear structural-equation/path analysis.
- **Measurement sequence:** work/person measures in 1964 and 1974, with earlier job-history indicators also used.
- **Key operationalization:** p. 1261 defines substantive complexity as work requiring thought and independent judgment. Occupational self-direction is not a class label; it is built from substantive complexity, freedom from close supervision, and low routinization/repetitiveness.
- **Retained result:** self-directed work predicts ideational flexibility and self-directed orientation; oppressive job conditions predict distress. Personality also predicts later position in job structure, especially movement toward more responsible and self-directed work.
- **Authors’ interpretation:** learning/generalization from recurrent job experience to life off the job.
- **Identification ceiling:** observational reciprocal model; do not turn “learning generalization” into an unobserved magical carrier or infer any individual psychological state from occupation alone.

### S3 — Miller, Schooler, Kohn & Miller 1979

- **Source:** Joanne Miller, Carmi Schooler, Melvin L. Kohn, and Karen A. Miller, “Women and Work: The Psychological Effects of Occupational Conditions,” *American Journal of Sociology* 85(1), 1979, pp. 66–94. DOI `10.1086/226974`; JSTOR `2778068`.
- **Inspection:** publisher abstract plus page-specific excerpts including pp. 66 and 82.
- **Claim status:** observational causal/path analysis.
- **Retained result:** among employed women, job conditions encouraging self-direction were associated with more effective intellectual functioning and flexible social orientation; pressures/uncertainties and constraints on self-direction were associated with less favorable psychological outcomes. Authors explicitly tested selection and several social circumstances and argued for reciprocal job↔person influence.
- **Boundary value:** important replication/generalization beyond the male panel used in the classic Kohn/Schooler longitudinal work.
- **Identification ceiling:** does not justify a sex→psychology prior; work conditions are the proposed causal inputs.

### S4 — Mortimer & Lorence 1979a

- **Source:** Jeylan T. Mortimer and Jon Lorence, “Work Experience and Occupational Value Socialization: A Longitudinal Study,” *American Journal of Sociology* 84(6), 1979, pp. 1361–1385. DOI `10.1086/226938`; JSTOR `2777896`.
- **Inspection:** publisher abstract/metadata.
- **Claim status:** 10-year longitudinal observational study of male college graduates; confirmatory factor analysis.
- **Retained result:** work autonomy and income predicted change in occupational reward values; earlier occupational values also predicted later work experience. Rewarding experience reinforced values implicated in earlier selection.
- **Identification ceiling:** selected educational cohort and observational design constrain generality and causal certainty.

### S5 — Mortimer & Lorence 1979b

- **Source:** Jeylan T. Mortimer and Jon Lorence, “Occupational Experience and the Self-Concept: A Longitudinal Study,” *Social Psychology Quarterly* 42(4), 1979, pp. 307–323. DOI `10.2307/3033802`; JSTOR `3033802`.
- **Inspection:** stable bibliographic record and abstract-level full-text description.
- **Claim status:** longitudinal observational panel; 435 male 1966–67 college graduates followed about 10 years.
- **Retained result:** work autonomy predicted a stronger sense of competence/personal efficacy; prior competence also had implications for later income attainment and work autonomy. With work autonomy controlled, income did not significantly enhance self-concept over time in the reported model.
- **Identification ceiling:** cohort-specific; work autonomy is not interchangeable with income or occupational status.

### S6 — Andrisani & Nestel 1976

- **Source:** Paul J. Andrisani and Gilbert Nestel, “Internal-External Control as Contributor to and Outcome of Work Experience,” *Journal of Applied Psychology* 61(2), 1976, pp. 156–165. DOI `10.1037/0021-9010.61.2.156`.
- **Inspection:** author/full-text abstract and bibliographic record.
- **Claim status:** longitudinal plus cross-sectional observational regression; National Longitudinal Surveys, `N=2,972`, representative national sample of middle-aged men.
- **Retained result:** internal control orientation predicted work success net of measured skills, abilities and demographic differences; work success also predicted movement toward greater internal control expectancy.
- **Identification ceiling:** “work success” is a broad outcome, not identical to autonomy, complexity, authority or class; residual selection remains possible.

### S7 — Pearlin, Menaghan, Lieberman & Mullan 1981

- **Source:** Leonard I. Pearlin, Elizabeth G. Menaghan, Morton A. Lieberman, and Joseph T. Mullan, “The Stress Process,” *Journal of Health and Social Behavior* 22(4), 1981, pp. 337–356. DOI `10.2307/2136676`; PMID `7320473`.
- **Inspection:** full text, especially pp. 338–342 and 344–352.
- **Claim status:** longitudinal observational panel with path models and interaction models.
- **Measurement sequence/sample:** interviews 1972–73 and 1976–77; original representative sample `N=2,300` adults aged 18–65 in the Chicago urbanized area; longitudinal follow-up `N=1,106`; authors report some disproportionate attrition among younger, nonwhite, low-income men.
- **Retained chain:** involuntary job disruption → reduced material resources/economic strain → erosion of self-esteem/mastery → increased depressive symptoms. Holding economic strain reduced the direct job-disruption→depression path, giving explicit partial mediation evidence.
- **Temporal result:** enduring strain is not the same thing as the event that precipitates it; strains can also exist without a precipitating event.
- **Mediator result:** coping and social support often work at antecedent junctures rather than as a generic direct buffer of depression; network membership alone does not equal received support.
- **Identification ceiling:** authors explicitly acknowledge alternative models, incomplete reciprocity, time-window dependence, and limited generality from the selected event/strain/outcome set.

### S8 — Pearlin 1989

- **Source:** Leonard I. Pearlin, “The Sociological Study of Stress,” *Journal of Health and Social Behavior* 30(3), 1989, pp. 241–256. DOI `10.2307/2136956`; PMID `2674272`.
- **Inspection:** PubMed abstract and bibliographic record.
- **Claim status:** critical review/theoretical synthesis.
- **Retained result:** statuses and institutional affiliations should be traced through differential exposure/meaning, access to mediators, and manifestations rather than merely used as controls; stress research should examine time-extended constellations of events and chronic strains.
- **Identification ceiling:** conceptual synthesis, not a new causal estimate.

### S9 — Pearlin, Aneshensel & LeBlanc 1997

- **Source:** Leonard I. Pearlin, Carol S. Aneshensel, and Allen J. LeBlanc, “The Forms and Mechanisms of Stress Proliferation: The Case of AIDS Caregivers,” *Journal of Health and Social Behavior* 38(3), 1997, pp. 223–236. DOI `10.2307/2955368`; PMID `9343962`.
- **Inspection:** PubMed abstract and stable bibliographic record.
- **Claim status:** empirical observational stress-process study.
- **Retained result:** primary caregiving hardships can expand into role overload/captivity as demands rise and can generate secondary stressors in social, leisure and occupational domains. Stress therefore propagates across roles instead of remaining attached to a single initiating condition.
- **Identification ceiling:** specific caregiving context; retain the propagation form, not AIDS caregiving as a universal template.

### S10 — House, Umberson & Landis 1988

- **Source:** James S. House, Debra Umberson, and Karl R. Landis, “Structures and Processes of Social Support,” *Annual Review of Sociology* 14, 1988, pp. 293–318. DOI `10.1146/annurev.so.14.080188.001453`.
- **Inspection:** publisher abstract/metadata.
- **Claim status:** review.
- **Retained distinction:** social integration/relationship quantity and network structure are distinct from support as the emotionally or instrumentally sustaining quality of relationships; relational demands/conflict and social regulation/control are additional processes.
- **Rawlsing value:** reinforces `NETWORK_TOPOLOGY != ACTUAL_SUPPORT_PROCESS`.

### S11 — Turner, Wheaton & Lloyd 1995

- **Source:** R. Jay Turner, Blair Wheaton, and Donald A. Lloyd, “The Epidemiology of Social Stress,” *American Sociological Review* 60(1), 1995, pp. 104–125. DOI `10.2307/2096348`.
- **Inspection:** indexed abstract/bibliographic record.
- **Claim status:** observational population study.
- **Retained result:** measured stress exposure was socially distributed, and exposure differences accounted for substantial portions of observed mental-health differences across several social statuses in their sample.
- **Identification ceiling:** group/status distribution evidence does not license direct status→individual distress or trait inference; it supports modeling differential exposure as an intervening empirical object.

### S12 — Wu, Griffin & Parker 2015

- **Source:** Chia-Huei Wu, Mark A. Griffin, and Sharon K. Parker, “Developing Agency through Good Work: Longitudinal Effects of Job Autonomy and Skill Utilization on Locus of Control,” *Journal of Vocational Behavior* 89, 2015, pp. 102–108. DOI `10.1016/j.jvb.2015.05.004`.
- **Inspection:** publisher/repository abstract and public author-copy locator.
- **Claim status:** three-wave longitudinal observational study over four years; HILDA `N=3,045`; latent difference-score modeling.
- **Retained result:** job autonomy predicted increased internal locus of control over time; job satisfaction also predicted change. Skill utilization did not independently predict locus-of-control change and the proposed indirect effects through satisfaction were weak.
- **Boundary value:** useful negative evidence against collapsing all “good work” dimensions into one effect.

### S13 — Staff & Mortimer 2024

- **Source:** Jeremy Staff and Jeylan T. Mortimer, “Work Experience and Mental Health from Adolescence to Mid-Life,” *Social Forces* 103(1), 2024, pp. 305–326. DOI `10.1093/sf/soae067`; PMCID `PMC11412419`.
- **Inspection:** full PMC manuscript, including methods, Tables 3–5, robustness checks and lagged/reverse-causality supplements.
- **Claim status:** prospective longitudinal observational cohort; 11 waves; fixed-effects models.
- **Sample/timing:** Youth Development Study cohort followed from ages 14–15 to 45–46; `N=1,007` / 8,555 person-waves for work status and `N=956` / 5,578 employed person-waves for work-quality analyses.
- **Retained result:** within-person increases in work self-direction, learning/skill opportunities and several extrinsic-quality measures were associated with higher mastery/self-esteem; increases in work stressors were associated with lower mastery/self-esteem and higher depressive affect. Fixed effects remove time-stable person confounding.
- **Critical qualification:** main models are contemporaneous/proximal because wave gaps average about 3.1 years. One-wave lagged work dimensions did not significantly predict later mental health; most reverse-lagged mental-health→work estimates were also null, with two exceptions involving advancement/learning opportunities. Authors explicitly say long and varying lags may obscure effects.
- **Identification ceiling:** fixed effects do not remove time-varying confounding, and contemporaneous association does not itself prove direction.

## 3. Source-saturation judgment

The T4 mechanism frontier is sufficiently saturated for Vozhd normalization around six recurring empirical forms:

1. organized work conditions → recurrent exercise/constraint of self-direction → psychological change;
2. psychological orientation/competence → later job selection/attainment → changed exposure;
3. discrete disruption → enduring material/role strain → self-concept/mastery change → distress;
4. chronic strain without a discrete event → cumulative psychological consequence;
5. coping/support as process-specific modifiers at particular causal junctures, not generic protective traits;
6. stress proliferation across roles/activities and socially patterned differential exposure.
