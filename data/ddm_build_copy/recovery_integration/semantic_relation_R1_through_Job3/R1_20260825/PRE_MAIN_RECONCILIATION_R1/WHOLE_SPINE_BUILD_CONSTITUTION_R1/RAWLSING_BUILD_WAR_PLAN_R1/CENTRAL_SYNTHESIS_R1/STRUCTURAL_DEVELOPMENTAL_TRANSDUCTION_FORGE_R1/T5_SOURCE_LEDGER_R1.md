## 0. Evidence discipline

This ledger was produced independently of T1-T4 and T6. Rawlsing mapping uses only the SDTF campaign commission and `01_CURRENT_PREV3_V3_SEAM_MAP_R1.md`; no sibling theory-lane return is consumed.

Claim-status labels used below:

- `THEORY` — conceptual/theoretical statement;
- `LONGITUDINAL` — repeated observations over persons/time;
- `OBSERVATIONAL` — nonexperimental empirical association/model;
- `QUALITATIVE` — life-history/narrative evidence;
- `REVIEW` — synthesis of prior literature;
- `CONTESTED/BOUNDED` — useful mechanism claim whose causal scope is explicitly limited.

The lane does not treat cohort membership, demographic category, or trajectory class as an individual psychological state.

## 1. Primary and strong empirical sources

### S01 — Elder 1994: canonical life-course principles

**SOURCE:** Glen H. Elder Jr. (1994), “Time, Human Agency, and Social Change: Perspectives on the Life Course,” *Social Psychology Quarterly* 57(1):4-15. DOI/JSTOR: `10.2307/2786971`.

**EXACT LOCATION:** pp. 5-7, especially p. 6 for the life-stage/timing principle and linked lives; article-wide pp. 4-15 for historical time, agency, and social change.

**STATUS:** `THEORY + PROGRAMMATIC REVIEW` grounded in longitudinal life-course research.

**RETAIN:** Historical change does not have one person-independent developmental meaning. Effects depend on life stage; lives are interdependent through kin/friend relations; agency selects and constructs courses within historically structured opportunity and constraint.

**CEILING:** The principles do not specify that a given age or cohort mechanically causes a trait.

### S02 — Elder 1998: life course as developmental theory

**SOURCE:** Glen H. Elder Jr. (1998), “The Life Course as Developmental Theory,” *Child Development* 69(1):1-12. DOI: `10.1111/j.1467-8624.1998.tb06128.x`; JSTOR `1132065`.

**EXACT LOCATION:** pp. 2-5 for the Depression-cohort comparisons and four core principles; p. 3 states historical time/place; pp. 3-4 develop timing, linked lives, and agency.

**STATUS:** `THEORY + LONGITUDINAL SYNTHESIS`.

**RETAIN:** The developmental impact of a succession of transitions/events is contingent on when it occurs; historical events reach persons through family and other linked relations; family adaptation changes the child’s realized ecology; choices occur inside available options and constraints.

**CEILING:** A historical event is not itself an actor exposure, uptake, or durable change.

### S03 — Elder & Rockwell 1979: developmental/social/historical age separation

**SOURCE:** Glen H. Elder Jr. & Richard C. Rockwell (1979), “The Life-Course and Human Development: An Ecological Perspective,” *International Journal of Behavioral Development* 2(1). DOI: `10.1177/016502547900200101`.

**EXACT LOCATION:** article abstract and opening framework distinguishing developmental, social, and historical meanings of age.

**STATUS:** `THEORY/REVIEW`.

**RETAIN:** Age is not a single causal variable; developmental age, age-graded social location, and historical cohort/location can alter exposure and meaning through different mechanisms.

**CEILING:** Chronological age alone is not a mechanism.

### S04 — Elder 1986: military service and timing-dependent turning points

**SOURCE:** Glen H. Elder Jr. (1986), “Military Times and Turning Points in Men’s Lives,” *Developmental Psychology* 22(2):233-245. DOI: `10.1037/0012-1649.22.2.233`.

**EXACT LOCATION:** pp. 233-245; hypotheses/results on early service among Berkeley men born 1928-1929 and subsequent life-course redirection.

**STATUS:** `LONGITUDINAL + OBSERVATIONAL`.

**RETAIN:** A common institutional transition can reorganize opportunity, training, independence, and later careers differently according to prior disadvantage and age/timing. Turning-point status is tied to consequential redirection, not merely event occurrence.

**CEILING:** Military service is not universally beneficial or a universal turning point; selection into service and historical period matter.

### S05 — Elder, Gimbel & Ivie 1991: subjective turning-point variation

**SOURCE:** Glen H. Elder Jr., Cynthia Gimbel & Rachel Ivie (1991), “Turning Points in Life: The Case of Military Service and War,” *Military Psychology* 3(4):215-231. DOI: `10.1207/s15327876mp0304_3`.

**EXACT LOCATION:** pp. 215-231; results relating perceived turning-point status to younger entry, Depression hardship, and successful military career.

**STATUS:** `LONGITUDINAL + RETROSPECTIVE/OBSERVATIONAL`.

**RETAIN:** Whether an event is experienced/remembered as a turning point depends on prior history, timing, and what the transition actually changes downstream.

**CEILING:** Subjective retrospective designation is not identical to objectively demonstrated causal redirection.

### S06 — Pavalko & Elder 1990: historical event × timing × relationship trajectory

**SOURCE:** Eliza K. Pavalko & Glen H. Elder Jr. (1990), “World War II and Divorce: A Life-Course Perspective,” *American Journal of Sociology* 95(5):1213-1234. DOI: `10.1086/229427`.

**EXACT LOCATION:** pp. 1213-1234; models distinguish military entry, timing of entry, combat experience, and marriage timing.

**STATUS:** `LONGITUDINAL + OBSERVATIONAL`.

**RETAIN:** The same macrohistorical period is decomposable into actor-specific transitions/exposures whose effects depend on timing relative to an existing relationship trajectory.

**CEILING:** A period effect cannot substitute for the realized service/combat/marital sequence.

### S07 — Mortimer et al. 1996: work intensity and prospective developmental outcomes

**SOURCE:** Jeylan T. Mortimer, Michael D. Finch, Seongryeol Ryu, Michael J. Shanahan & Kathleen T. Call (1996), “The Effects of Work Intensity on Adolescent Mental Health, Achievement, and Behavioral Adjustment: New Evidence from a Prospective Study,” *Child Development* 67(3):1243-1261. DOI: `10.1111/j.1467-8624.1996.tb01793.x`.

**EXACT LOCATION:** pp. 1243-1261; four-year prospective panel of roughly 1,000 ninth graders, with lagged/control models.

**STATUS:** `LONGITUDINAL + OBSERVATIONAL`.

**RETAIN:** Duration/intensity of a recurrent activity matters and cannot be replaced by a binary “worked/did not work” event. Selection and socialization must be modeled separately.

**CEILING:** Work intensity is not a universal developmental cause across outcomes; the study itself reports outcome-specific effects.

### S08 — Mortimer et al. 1996: work quality, learning opportunity, occupational values

**SOURCE:** Jeylan T. Mortimer, Ellen Efron Pimentel, Seongryeol Ryu, Katherine Nash & Chaimun Lee (1996), “Part-Time Work and Occupational Value Formation in Adolescence,” *Social Forces* 74(4):1405-1418. DOI: `10.1093/sf/74.4.1405`.

**EXACT LOCATION:** pp. 1405-1418; four-year panel, especially models contrasting employment/hours with opportunity to learn useful skills.

**STATUS:** `LONGITUDINAL + OBSERVATIONAL`.

**RETAIN:** The quality/content of recurrent activity can matter more than category membership or raw duration. Opportunity to learn useful skills predicts change in increasingly stable work-value dimensions.

**CEILING:** “Worker” is not a psychological trait; employment status/hours alone did not carry the main developmental result.

### S09 — Mortimer, Harley & Staff 2002: quality of work and mental health

**SOURCE:** Jeylan T. Mortimer, Carolyn Harley & Jeremy Staff (2002), “The Quality of Work and Youth Mental Health,” *Work and Occupations* 29(2):166-197. DOI: `10.1177/0730888402029002003`.

**EXACT LOCATION:** pp. 166-197; contemporaneous and longer-term models distinguishing work quality and selection/socialization.

**STATUS:** `LONGITUDINAL + OBSERVATIONAL`.

**RETAIN:** Recurrent work conditions can have contemporaneous psychological consequences while showing weak long-term persistence. Durable change must therefore be earned rather than inferred from exposure.

**CEILING:** A contemporaneous state shift does not license a permanent trait update.

### S10 — O’Rand 1996: institutional stratification × work/pension history

**SOURCE:** Angela M. O’Rand (1996), “The Precious and the Precocious: Understanding Cumulative Disadvantage and Cumulative Advantage Over the Life Course,” *The Gerontologist* 36(2):230-238. DOI: `10.1093/geront/36.2.230`.

**EXACT LOCATION:** pp. 230-238; especially discussion of labor markets/pensions interacting with labor-force and job-mobility histories.

**STATUS:** `THEORY/REVIEW + EMPIRICAL STRATIFICATION SYNTHESIS`.

**RETAIN:** Cumulative inequality is produced through long-run interaction between institutional allocation/reward rules and realized work/employer/pension histories. Institutional rules do not become outcomes without the actor’s actual sequence of positions and transitions.

**CEILING:** Cross-sectional inequality among older people does not reveal the process that produced it.

### S11 — Dannefer 1987: intracohort differentiation

**SOURCE:** Dale Dannefer (1987), “Aging as Intracohort Differentiation: Accentuation, the Matthew Effect, and the Life Course,” *Sociological Forum* 2(2):211-236. DOI: `10.1007/BF01124164`.

**EXACT LOCATION:** pp. 211-236.

**STATUS:** `THEORY/REVIEW`.

**RETAIN:** Aging populations can become more heterogeneous through socially organized differentiation, so increasing between-person variance over time is itself a phenomenon requiring a social process account.

**CEILING:** Heterogeneity with age is not proof of one specific cumulative mechanism.

### S12 — Dannefer 2003: cumulative advantage/disadvantage as time-dependent process

**SOURCE:** Dale Dannefer (2003), “Cumulative Advantage/Disadvantage and the Life Course: Cross-Fertilizing Age and Social Science Theory,” *Journals of Gerontology Series B* 58(6):S327-S337. DOI: `10.1093/geronb/58.6.S327`.

**EXACT LOCATION:** S327-S337.

**STATUS:** `THEORY + REVIEW`.

**RETAIN:** Cumulative advantage/disadvantage is inherently temporal and concerns processes that amplify or differentiate positions/outcomes through successive allocation and exposure, not a static “advantaged/disadvantaged” label.

**CEILING:** A cumulative label does not identify the allocation/feedback steps; Rawlsing must retain those steps explicitly.

### S13 — Ferraro & Shippee 2009: cumulative inequality axioms

**SOURCE:** Kenneth F. Ferraro & Tetyana Pylypiv Shippee (2009), “Aging and Cumulative Inequality: How Does Inequality Get Under the Skin?” *The Gerontologist* 49(3):333-343. DOI: `10.1093/geront/gnp034`; PMCID `PMC2721665`.

**EXACT LOCATION:** pp. 334-337 for “Axioms of CI Theory” and Table 1; especially axiom 2 (risk/opportunity exposure), axiom 3 (risk/resources/agency), axiom 4 (perceived trajectories), axiom 5 (mortality selection).

**STATUS:** `THEORY + REVIEW`.

**RETAIN:** Iteration must include differential future exposure to risk/opportunity; onset, duration, and magnitude matter; resource mobilization and turning points may alter chains; nonrandom mortality/attrition can distort observed late-life inequality.

**CEILING:** Early disadvantage is probabilistic, modifiable, and not destiny; population composition is not an individual developmental prior.

### S14 — Laub & Sampson 1993: turning points and cumulative continuity

**SOURCE:** John H. Laub & Robert J. Sampson (1993), “Turning Points in the Life Course: Why Change Matters to the Study of Crime,” *Criminology* 31(3):301-325. DOI: `10.1111/j.1745-9125.1993.tb01132.x`.

**EXACT LOCATION:** pp. 301-325; conceptual separation of self-selection, cumulative developmental continuity, adult social bonds/social capital, and change.

**STATUS:** `THEORY + LONGITUDINAL/QUALITATIVE ILLUSTRATION` using Glueck life histories.

**RETAIN:** Prior behavior can weaken later institutional/relationship bonds, producing a feedback chain; conversely, changes in adult bonds/routines can redirect later behavior. A turning point is a transition whose changed bindings and routines alter subsequent trajectory.

**CEILING:** Marriage/employment are not universally causal turning points; selection and bond quality/realized routine matter.

### S15 — DiPrete & Eirich 2006: mechanism review of cumulative advantage

**SOURCE:** Thomas A. DiPrete & Gregory M. Eirich (2006), “Cumulative Advantage as a Mechanism for Inequality: A Review of Theoretical and Empirical Developments,” *Annual Review of Sociology* 32:271-297. DOI: `10.1146/annurev.soc.32.061604.123127`.

**EXACT LOCATION:** pp. 271-297.

**STATUS:** `REVIEW`.

**RETAIN:** “Cumulative advantage” covers more than one generative process; architectural use must name the actual feedback/allocation mechanism rather than treating the umbrella term as executable semantics.

**CEILING:** No generic Matthew-effect operator should be created without a domain-specific transition/update rule.

## 2. Source-strength disposition

`PRIMARY/CORE`: S01, S02, S04-S14.

`STRONG COMPARATOR/REVIEW`: S03, S15.

No retained source licenses `SOCIAL CATEGORY -> TRAIT`. The strongest empirical records instead repeatedly require realized activity/relationship/institutional transitions, timing, intensity/quality, prior history, and selection/agency before developmental consequences can be asserted.
