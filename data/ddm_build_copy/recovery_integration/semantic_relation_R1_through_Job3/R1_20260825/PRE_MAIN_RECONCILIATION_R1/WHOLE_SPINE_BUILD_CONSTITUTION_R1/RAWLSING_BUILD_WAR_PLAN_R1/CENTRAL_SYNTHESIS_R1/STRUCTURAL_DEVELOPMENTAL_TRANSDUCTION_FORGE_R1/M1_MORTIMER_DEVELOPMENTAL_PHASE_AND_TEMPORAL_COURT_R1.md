## 2. Strongest phase evidence

### 2.1 Formal autonomy/control is not phase-invariant in psychological implication

**Source:** Ch. 6, roughly pp. 154–160, especially the discussion of work quality and depressive affect.

Mortimer first notes that employment itself and current work intensity show scant consistent relation to the major mental-health indicators. The important variation lies instead in work quality and in how teenage jobs differ from adult work: adolescents usually do not depend on the job for basic subsistence, their jobs are more transitory, and the same nominal adultlike condition can have different significance in a novice teenage work role.

The YDS results discussed in this section report that work self-direction/control for adolescent boys can relate to greater depressed affect rather than the adult pattern expected from the Kohn/Schooler literature. Mortimer interprets this cautiously: novice workers may possess less role knowledge and problem-solving experience, so decision authority/responsibility can become burden rather than mastery, especially in high-intensity work. This is an author interpretation of a selection-controlled longitudinal association, not randomized proof of a universal sign reversal.

**Court decomposition:**

```text
FORMAL DECISION AUTHORITY
+ ADOLESCENT/NOVICE PHASE
+ LIMITED PRIOR WORK HISTORY / ROLE KNOWLEDGE
+ HIGH HOURS OR AMBIGUITY
-> ENACTED RESPONSIBILITY/BURDEN
-> SUBJECT-SPECIFIC UPTAKE
-> DISTRESS ASSOCIATION
```

versus the adult literature's common pathway:

```text
FORMAL DECISION AUTHORITY
+ MATURE/EXPERIENCED WORK HISTORY
+ COMPETENCE / ROLE KNOWLEDGE
-> EXERCISED SELF-DIRECTION
-> DIFFERENT PSYCHOLOGICAL CONSEQUENCES
```

The result forbids:

`FORMAL AUTONOMY -> SAME EXPERIENCE -> SAME EFFECT AT EVERY DEVELOPMENTAL PHASE`.

It supports the frozen D1 realization rule that formal autonomy must be converted into an **enacted task/supervision state** before an exercised-autonomy condition exists, and the frozen D2/phase-history requirement that the conversion be indexed to prior history and active phase.

### 2.2 Institutional age rules change the exposure ecology itself

**Source:** Ch. 3, pp. 54–56, Table 3.1.

Employed boys' mean weekly hours rise from 11.3 in ninth grade to 19.6 in tenth grade; the median rises from 7.5 to 20. Mortimer notes that by tenth grade most youth had turned 16, the age when Minnesota temporal restrictions on youth work were loosened.

This is not an effect of “developmental phase” alone. It is an exact example of:

```text
AGE/PHASE
+ INSTITUTIONAL RULE THRESHOLD
-> CHANGED LEGAL SCHEDULE POSSIBILITY
-> CHANGED ACTOR-RELATIVE HOURS ECOLOGY
```

So a phase marker that does not bind the relevant rule/world/context would be causally empty.

### 2.3 The same prior work history has different observed family-transition significance at different later ages

**Source:** Ch. 7, pp. 196–200, Figs. 7.2–7.3 and Table 7.3.

At ages 21–22, high-school work-investment patterns had no significant bearing on residential independence, marriage/cohabitation, or parenthood. At ages 24–25, the more invested adolescent workers were more likely to be married/cohabiting, and high-investment histories were associated with parenthood. Mortimer explicitly interprets the later timing as more normative rather than “precocious maturity.”

This is not evidence that the earlier work history changed its physical content after the fact. It shows that the later **opportunity/gate/normative ecology** and the actor's active life phase alter which transition is available and how a prior history participates in it.

Court rule:

`PRIOR EXPOSURE HISTORY + DIFFERENT CURRENT PHASE/GATE ECOLOGY != SAME TRANSITION PROBABILITY`.

## 4. Duration and intensity court

Mortimer's work-history construction directly demonstrates:

```text
DURATION != INTENSITY != CUMULATIVE HOURS != SNAPSHOT EMPLOYMENT
```

The “steady” pattern is long duration/low-to-moderate intensity. The “sporadic” pattern is shorter duration/high intensity. The “most invested” pattern is long duration/high intensity. These patterns predict later outcomes differently; therefore a temporal representation that stores only total hours loses causally relevant ordering/recurrence information.

**D2 verdict:** `STRONGLY CONFIRMED, WITH PARAMETER CLARIFICATION.`

Required D2 parameters exposed by Mortimer:

- active interval start/end;
- weekly intensity within interval;
- recurrence/persistence across intervals;
- episode sequence;
- cross-domain schedule conflict or compatibility;
- transition points with post-transition rebinding;
- target-specific persistence/attenuation after exposure ends.

No additional temporal primitive is licensed.

## 5. Persistent-change court

Mortimer repeatedly separates short-run from durable outcome:

- high-intensity work is associated with more adolescent drinking/problem behavior;
- four years after high school, work-investment groups no longer differ in drinking;
- later depressive-affect effects of adolescent work quality are few;
- intensive workers' early earnings advantage substantially converges by the later observation;
- steady work remains associated with B.A. attainment under substantial controls.

Therefore:

```text
CONDITION/RECURRENCE
-> CONTEMPORANEOUS RESPONSE
!=
PERSISTENT V3 CHANGE
```

The seam must preserve temporal provenance and hand the result to V3; it cannot mark durability merely because an exposure recurred.
