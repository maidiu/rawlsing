## 0. Source discipline

Primary source inspected in full:

Melvin L. Kohn and Carmi Schooler, with Joanne Miller, Karen A. Miller, Carrie Schoenbach, and Ronald Schoenberg, *Work and Personality: An Inquiry into the Impact of Social Stratification* (Ablex, 1983).

The project source is the complete scanned 414-page book. Page references below use the book's printed pagination. The source was read as a book, including the preface, all thirteen chapters, methods/measurement appendices, tables/figures where text extraction permitted exact recovery, and the authors' own methodological cautions. No review, abstract, or later source is substituted for a passage available in the book.

Evidence labels used here:

- `THEORY/INTERPRETATION` — causal interpretation or mechanism proposed by the authors.
- `CROSS_SECTIONAL_ASSOCIATION` — association in the 1964 survey or another one-time sample.
- `CROSS_SECTIONAL_STRUCTURAL_EQUATION` — reciprocal model estimated from one-time data with identifying restrictions.
- `LONGITUDINAL_SEM` — 1964/1974 reciprocal structural-equation analysis.
- `REPORTED_BEHAVIOR` — respondent report of actual behavior outside work rather than an intrapsychic scale.
- `REPLICATION/REVIEW` — evidence summarized from other studies/countries.
- `NULL/BOUNDARY` — explicit null, failed measure, weak identification, or generalization ceiling.

No `LONGITUDINAL_SEM` result is upgraded here to randomized causal identification.

## 1. Preface (pp. viii-xii): research program and evidentiary hierarchy

The program began with an attempted explanation of differences between middle-class and working-class parental values through education and occupational experience, especially opportunity to exercise self-direction. The book deliberately broadens this into a test of concrete job conditions and multiple, distinct psychological outcomes.

The authors identify Section III as the heart of the book because it uses the 1974 follow-up to test reciprocal effects. This matters for M6: their own evidentiary hierarchy distinguishes the early cross-sectional demonstrations from the later longitudinal reciprocal models.

Retained source law:

`CLASS/STRATIFICATION LABEL -> PERSONALITY` is not the book's mature explanatory mechanism. The mature program asks which concrete occupational conditions covary with structural position, whether those conditions affect psychological functioning, and whether psychological functioning reciprocally changes later occupational conditions.

## 2. Chapter 1, “Stratification, Occupation, and Orientation” (pp. 3-33)

### 2.1 Social stratification is not social class

By pp. 21-22 the authors treat social stratification as a continuous hierarchy, centrally indexed by education and occupational status in the original analysis. They later reserve `social class` for a specifically Marxian relationship to ownership/control of the means of production. Income and subjective class identification contribute much less once education/occupational status are controlled in the early analysis.

Evidence grade: `CROSS_SECTIONAL_ASSOCIATION + CONCEPTUAL_DECOMPOSITION`.

### 2.2 Occupational self-direction is a second-order construct, not a primitive mechanism

At pp. 22-27 the authors identify three distinct conditions that facilitate/restrict occupational self-direction:

1. **closeness of supervision** — a limiting condition on discretion;
2. **substantive complexity** — whether the work itself requires initiative, thought, and independent judgment;
3. **complexity of organization / nonroutinization** — whether work permits/requires varied approaches rather than repeated performance of the same task in the same way.

They explicitly state that no one of these conditions is definitionally identical to occupational self-direction. Together they largely determine how much self-direction can be exercised.

This is decisive for M6: `OCCUPATIONAL_SELF_DIRECTION` is an analytically useful latent/composite summary, but it is not a self-explaining causal operator.

### 2.3 Actual work content was elicited independently of title

At pp. 23-26 interviewers asked what respondents actually did, how much time they spent reading/writing, working with hands/tools/machines, and dealing with people, and then probed the exact operations, materials, tools, machine setups, and interactions. Complexity and time allocation mattered more than occupational labels or the surface identity of the task.

Evidence grade: `MEASUREMENT_DESIGN + CROSS_SECTIONAL_ASSOCIATION`.

M6 candidate invariant:

`OCCUPATION_TITLE != WORK_CONTENT`.

### 2.4 Routinization/repetition

At pp. 26-27 the authors distinguish endlessly repetitive work from work with varied tasks or varied ways of performing similar tasks. Their questions explicitly ask whether the respondent does the same thing in the same way repeatedly, the same kind of thing in different ways, or different kinds of things.

Mechanism proposed: simply organized/repetitive work limits the occasions on which initiative, thought, and judgment are required; varied/unpredictable work creates more such occasions.

Evidence grade at this stage: `CROSS_SECTIONAL_ASSOCIATION + THEORY`.

### 2.5 Other job conditions

The chapter also identifies ownership, competition, job security, bureaucratic size/supervisory structure, hierarchical position, time pressure and overtime as independently related to some outcomes. They are not, however, as central to the stratification explanation as the three conditions that determine occupational self-direction.

## 3. Chapter 2, “Bureaucratic Man” (pp. 34-54)

This chapter tests whether bureaucratization itself is the psychological engine. The mature implication carried forward to later chapters is negative: organizational scale/bureaucratic location matters largely by changing more proximal job conditions, particularly substantive complexity, protections/rewards, hierarchy and other conditions of work. Bureaucratization is therefore not a permission to infer a psychological state directly from an organizational category.

Evidence grade: `CROSS_SECTIONAL_ASSOCIATION + MEDIATION_INTERPRETATION`.

M6 use: organizational form may be a structural input to D1, not a psychological output.

## 4. Chapter 3, “Occupational Experience and Psychological Functioning: An Assessment of Reciprocal Effects” (pp. 55-81)

This is the first explicit test of the rival explanations:

- occupational experience affects psychological functioning;
- selective recruitment puts already-different people into different jobs;
- incumbents mold jobs to fit themselves.

Using 1964 cross-sectional data and simultaneous-equation/two-stage least-squares procedures, the chapter finds evidence consistent with effects in both directions.

Evidence grade: `CROSS_SECTIONAL_STRUCTURAL_EQUATION`.

Ceiling: the chapter cannot establish temporal ordering because it lacks prior measures of psychological functioning and many prior job conditions. The authors themselves later treat it as prima facie evidence that justified the follow-up, not as the final causal court.

## 5. Chapter 4, “Occupational Structure and Alienation” (pp. 82-98)

This chapter decomposes ownership, hierarchical position, bureaucratic organization, occupational self-direction, and alienation-related outcomes. A central result is that ownership and hierarchy lose much explanatory power once occupational self-direction and other proximal conditions are entered.

M6 implication: formal control over product or organizational rank cannot stand in for actual control over the labor process or the recurrent conditions under which work is done.

Evidence grade: `CROSS_SECTIONAL_ASSOCIATION + DECOMPOSITION`.

## 6. Chapter 5, “The Reciprocal Effects of the Substantive Complexity of Work, and Intellectual Flexibility” (pp. 103-124)

This is the strongest single source in the book for the recurrent-activity seam.

### 6.1 Construct

At pp. 105-109 substantive complexity is defined as the degree to which performance itself requires thought and independent judgment, particularly decisions involving ill-defined or apparently conflicting contingencies. It is measured from the respondent's detailed description of what he does, not from occupation title alone. Multiple indicators cover overall complexity and complexity/time of work with data, people, and things.

### 6.2 Reciprocal longitudinal design

The model uses 1964 and 1974 measures, plus pre-1964 substantive complexity. Exogenous/background controls include age, education, parental education, father's occupational level, maternal and paternal grandfathers' occupational levels, race, national background, religion, region/urban-rural upbringing, and other relevant social background variables.

Identification requires exclusion restrictions/instruments. The authors explicitly discuss the identifying assumptions rather than treating the SEM as design-free causality.

### 6.3 Main paths

The full model estimates a statistically significant contemporaneous effect of 1974 substantive complexity on 1974 ideational flexibility of about `.18`; the corresponding 1964 -> 1974 cross-lag is small/nonsignificant (`.05`). The simplified/confirmed model gives the contemporaneous complexity -> flexibility path as `.17`.

The reciprocal effect is strongly lagged: earlier ideational flexibility predicts later substantive complexity. In the simplified model the path is about `.45`; in the combined measurement/causal validation it is about `.41`.

The authors' interpretation is asymmetric in timing: job demands affect current thinking relatively rapidly; ideational flexibility changes job complexity only over a longer period.

Evidence grade: `LONGITUDINAL_SEM__STRONG_OBSERVATIONAL`.

Identification ceiling: two waves ten years apart do not reveal the actual onset, frequency, dose-response, action pathway, employer gate, or whether a particular later job difference came from promotion, job change, job redesign, or another process.

### 6.4 Dynamic amplification

The chapter explicitly entertains a recursive developmental process: early differences in complexity may produce intellectual changes that contribute to movement toward later jobs of differing complexity, which then produce further differences.

This is source support for a loop of the form:

`WORK_CONTENT_t -> PERSON_t+1 -> LATER_WORK_CONTENT_t+2 -> PERSON_t+3`.

But the action/gate between person and later job content is inferred, not directly measured.

## 7. Chapter 6, “Job Conditions and Personality: A Longitudinal Assessment of Their Reciprocal Effects” (pp. 125-153)

### 7.1 Fourteen structural imperatives

At pp. 126-129 the authors distinguish:

- organizational position: ownership, bureaucratization, hierarchical position;
- occupational self-direction: substantive complexity, closeness of supervision, routinization;
- job pressures: time pressure, heaviness, dirtiness, hours worked;
- extrinsic risks/rewards: responsibility for matters outside one's control, risk of job/business loss, job protections, job income.

They call these `structural imperatives` because they are built into the job and linked to its location in economic/social structure; they are realities the incumbent confronts.

### 7.2 Measurement of self-direction

Occupational self-direction is explicitly defined as use of initiative, thought and independent judgment. Substantive complexity is task demand; supervision is a restriction on opportunity; routinization is repetitive/predictable task organization. The three are modeled together but remain separable first-order conditions.

Important measurement null: the earlier second routinization indicator (“unit of work”) cross-loaded on substantive complexity and supervision, so the final longitudinal model uses essentially a single routinization indicator augmented with predictability information.

### 7.3 Identification strategy

With only two waves and many endogenous job conditions, the full model cannot freely estimate both contemporaneous and cross-lagged effects for every pair. The authors give priority to contemporaneous job-structure relations and, after nonsignificant contemporaneous paths are fixed to zero, test corresponding lagged effects. Ownership, bureaucratization and hierarchical position cannot be contemporaneously caused by other variables in the model because changing them implies movement to a different structural position/job.

This is a major evidence ceiling: a label `LAGGED` in these models is not a randomized transition mechanism, and a `CONTEMPORANEOUS` effect may encompass processes occurring at unknown times between interviews.

### 7.4 Findings

Substantive complexity remains the most important work condition for ideational flexibility. Routinization and close supervision have smaller direct and/or indirect effects. Earlier ideational flexibility predicts later substantive complexity, and it also predicts later supervision conditions; the authors interpret these person -> job effects as delayed selection/molding rather than instant structural change.

Self-directedness of orientation and distress are separate psychological dimensions. The broad model shows substantial work -> person effects on both, but the exact job conditions differ by outcome. Distress is especially tied to oppressive/aversive conditions such as weak job protections, dirtiness and close supervision, not simply to “class.”

Evidence grade: `LONGITUDINAL_SEM__BROAD_BUT_IDENTIFICATION_CONSTRAINED`.

## 8. Chapter 7, “Class, Stratification, and Psychological Functioning” (pp. 154-190)

### 8.1 Social-stratification measurement

The chapter models social-stratification position as shared variance among occupational status, education, and job income. The authors explicitly warn that standard occupational-status scores already encode educational/income information and may overstate the status loading. The latent factor describes covariation; it does not itself explain mechanism.

### 8.2 Occupational position is not occupational self-direction

Disaggregated models show occupational status losing or reversing much of its apparent psychological association after education, income and concrete self-direction conditions are modeled. One or more of substantive complexity, closeness of supervision and routinization remains relevant for every major psychological outcome in the decomposition.

Retained implication:

`OCCUPATIONAL_STATUS != ACTUAL_JOB_CONDITIONS != OCCUPATIONAL_SELF_DIRECTION`.

### 8.3 Class

The chapter reserves class for ownership/control relations rather than a status gradient. It intentionally refuses to define class by occupational autonomy because autonomy/self-direction is to be explained as a consequence/mediator of class position, not folded into the class definition.

This is direct source support for keeping structural category and actor-relative work condition separate.

### 8.4 Reciprocal occupational-self-direction models

Table 7.4 reports statistically significant work -> person paths across many outcomes, with reciprocal person -> occupational-self-direction paths for some but not all outcomes. Examples:

- parental valuation of self-direction: OSD -> value `.14`, value -> OSD `.15`;
- valuation of self-direction for oneself: `.32`, reciprocal `-.05` nonsignificant;
- ideational flexibility: `.27`, reciprocal `.63`;
- self-directedness of orientation: `.43`, reciprocal `.28`;
- distress: `-.21`, reciprocal `-.04` nonsignificant;
- alienation: `-.30`, reciprocal `-.12`;
- authoritarian-conservatism: `-.29`, reciprocal `-.21`;
- trustfulness: `.22`, reciprocal `.01` nonsignificant;
- anxiety: `-.23`, reciprocal `-.02` nonsignificant.

These coefficients arise from constrained longitudinal SEM and must not be read as randomized treatment effects.

### 8.5 A rejected mediation hypothesis

The chapter rejects the hypothesis that ideational flexibility is the general mediator through which occupational self-direction changes values/orientations. In the multivariate models, flexibility does not play the expected intervening role for several value/orientation outcomes. Self-directed orientation is more pivotal in some of these relationships.

This is important for Rawlsing: do not create a generic `COMPLEXITY -> COGNITIVE FLEXIBILITY -> ALL OTHER PERSONALITY` pipeline.

## 9. Chapter 8, “Women and Work” (pp. 195-216)

The female sample consists of wives interviewed at follow-up; 555 women were interviewed and 269 who worked at least about ten hours per week in paid employment form the employed-women analysis. These are mostly married women linked to the male follow-up sample, not a nationally representative longitudinal panel of women.

Substantive complexity and routinization show relationships with ideational flexibility and self-directedness; closeness of supervision is less consistent for women than for men. Reciprocal causal models are necessarily weaker because there are no 1964 measures of the women's psychological functioning.

Evidence grade: `CROSS_SECTIONAL/QUASI-CAUSAL_EXTENSION__NOT_EQUIVALENT_TO_MALE_LONGITUDINAL_EVIDENCE`.

Generalization ceiling: the book cannot lawfully treat gender as a psychological prior; it shows that actual job conditions matter, but the women's design is weaker and the sample composition limits external generalization.

## 10. Chapter 9, “The Reciprocal Effects of Job Conditions and the Intellectuality of Leisure-Time Activities” (pp. 217-241)

This chapter is the strongest direct test that work experience generalizes into nonwork behavior.

Leisure-time intellectuality is constructed from reported activities (e.g. intellectually demanding reading, cultural activities, hobbies, strategic sports, television pattern), not simply from a self-description of being intellectual.

For men and women, substantive complexity positively predicts leisure-time intellectuality; hours worked is negative, and income/hierarchical position have smaller or specification-sensitive roles. For men, earlier leisure-time intellectuality also predicts later substantive complexity and some other job conditions.

The authors conclude that learning-generalization and facilitation/constraint explain much of the pattern; they find little support for pure compartmentalization and limited/equivocal support for compensation.

Crucial negative result: ideational flexibility does not explain away the work -> leisure association. The carryover therefore is not demonstrated to operate only through a latent cognitive trait; it may involve generalization of patterns of activity themselves.

Evidence grade: `LONGITUDINAL_SEM + REPORTED_NONWORK_BEHAVIOR` for men; `QUASI-LONGITUDINAL/SENSITIVITY` for women.

## 11. Chapter 10, “Housework as Work” (pp. 242-260)

The chapter tests whether the work-content logic travels outside paid employment.

For women, substantive complexity of housework is associated with ideational flexibility and self-directedness, and some structural-equation estimates are consistent with housework -> psychological effects. However, the evidence is cross-sectional and weakly identified.

Critical measurement failure: the proposed household analogue to closeness of supervision did not work. Questions about a spouse's comments/directions did not yield a defensible measure of “closeness of supervision” in housework.

Critical heterogeneity: the housework-complexity pattern found for women does not reproduce straightforwardly for men.

Evidence grade: `CROSS_SECTIONAL_ASSOCIATION + WEAKLY_IDENTIFIED_STRUCTURAL_EQUATION + NULLS`.

## 12. Chapter 11, “Serfdom's Legacy: An Ethnic Continuum” (pp. 261-278)

This chapter uses a cross-sectional historical/ethnic index to argue that cultural residues of historically constrained autonomy may persist across generations. It is the most dangerous chapter for Rawlsing if read incautiously.

The chapter cannot observe each link from historical legal/economic regime -> family/cultural practice -> individual experience -> psychological functioning. The authors explicitly acknowledge that they cannot confirm each causal link and frame the explanation as tentative.

M6 disposition: `EVIDENCE_ONLY/DEFER_AS_ARCHITECTURAL_SUPPORT`. Do not infer actor psychology from ethnicity or ancestral category. At most the chapter motivates investigation of provenance-bearing intergenerational social mechanisms, which are not directly measured here.

## 13. Chapter 12, “The Cross-National Universality of the Interpretive Model” (pp. 281-295)

This chapter reviews replications, especially Poland and Japan. The broadest support concerns values, social orientations and cognitive functioning. Self-conception results are weaker and not cross-nationally universal. Distress also varies more by social/historical context than the core self-direction/flexibility results.

Important measurement warning (p. 289 n.5): occupation-level Dictionary of Occupational Titles approximations to substantive complexity may be useful when individual job data are absent, but they “seriously underestimate” relationships because they replace actual job content with occupation-average content.

This strongly reinforces the M6 `OCCUPATION_TITLE != WORK_CONTENT` court.

Evidence grade: `REPLICATION/REVIEW`.

## 14. Chapter 13, “Unresolved Interpretive Issues” (pp. 296-314)

This chapter is a source of negative ceilings, not an appendix to be ignored.

### 14.1 Objective conditions versus subjective appraisals

At pp. 297-299 the authors emphasize objective job conditions — what a worker actually does, who determines how, the physical/social circumstances, risks and rewards — but acknowledge that their measures are interview reports and not direct observation. They call for designs that measure both objective conditions and subjective appraisals and ask how objective conditions become subjective experience.

### 14.2 Interpersonal context/support

They acknowledge undermeasurement of coworker/supervisor relations and possible buffering/support interactions.

### 14.3 Learning-generalization

At pp. 300-301 they describe the predominant interpretation as direct learning-generalization: complex/self-directed work is carried over into broader ways of thinking, valuing and acting. But they explicitly state that the timing is unresolved.

### 14.4 Timing/duration

The ten-year two-wave design cannot show when effects begin, whether they are continuous/discontinuous, taper off, accumulate, or require thresholds. Some effects may occur in anticipation; others may habituate; others may grow more onerous with duration.

This is decisive for D2: the book strongly supports preserving temporal form but does not identify an exact dose/recurrence law.

### 14.5 Subgroup heterogeneity

The authors state that many subgroup interactions remain insufficiently tested. “Average” effects must not be converted into universal deterministic effects.

### 14.6 Family transmission

At pp. 309-310 the authors explicitly reject the assumption that parental values are automatically reproduced in children. Parent-child value correlations in cited studies are modest; the mechanism is embedded in home, school, and wider social conditions. The book does not establish a simple parent-value -> child-value transmission law.

### 14.7 Modification of job conditions

At pp. 310-314 they distinguish observational evidence about jobs from the question of deliberately changing job structure. Their results do not establish how institutions can be redesigned or how easy/difficult such change is.

## 15. Appendix A, “Methods of the 1964 Cross-Sectional Survey and 1974 Follow-Up Survey” (pp. 315-320)

### 15.1 Baseline

- area-probability sample: `N=3,101` men;
- U.S. men age 16+;
- employed in civilian occupations at least 25 hours/week;
- unemployed and military excluded;
- women excluded from the 1964 sample;
- overall response rate about `76%`;
- nonresponse was higher in the largest metropolitan areas;
- sample somewhat older/better educated/more often married than Census “employed males” because of selection criteria.

### 15.2 Follow-up

- follow-up sampling frame restricted to the `2,553` baseline men younger than 55 in 1964 so they would generally still be in the labor force in 1974;
- `687` men were reinterviewed, `78%` of those selected for follow-up;
- reinterviewed men were slightly more intellectually flexible/trustful, slightly lower in self-confidence, and somewhat more liberal in religious background at baseline, but did not differ significantly on most major occupational/stratification variables;
- major baseline substantive relationships reproduced in the follow-up subsample.

These facts limit generalization to employed civilian men and make retirement/unemployment transitions underrepresented by design.

## 16. Appendix B, “Classifications of the Complexity of Work” (pp. 321-325)

The appendix is direct evidence that substantive complexity denotes specific activity demands rather than prestige.

### Data

Increasing levels include reading instructions, comparing, copying, computing, compiling, analyzing, coordinating, and synthesizing. The upper levels require evaluating alternatives, modifying designs, deciding goals/policies/procedures, integrating analyses, discovering/developing knowledge/concepts, and inventing new approaches.

### Things

Increasing complexity runs from handling/feed-offbearing/tending through manipulating, driving-operating, operating-controlling, precision working and setting up. High levels involve monitoring multiple variables, frequent adjustment and considerable judgment.

### People

Increasing complexity includes serving/helping, speaking/signaling, persuading, diverting, supervising, instructing, negotiating and mentoring.

### Overall job complexity

The highest levels are characterized by complex problem solving, many variables, unpredictable contingencies, analysis/synthesis, originality, insight and relationships whose outcomes cannot be specified mechanically in advance.

M6 implication: the developmental candidate is repeated engagement in forms of judgment/problem solving/coordination, not occupational prestige or title.

## 17. Appendices C-D: longitudinal measurement models

These appendices document that the main longitudinal constructs are latent measurement models with correlated residuals and imperfect indicators. They improve measurement-error handling but do not turn a latent score into a mechanism and do not solve causal identification by themselves.

## 18. Full-book source verdict

The source most strongly supports this decomposed causal grammar:

```text
STRUCTURAL / ORGANIZATIONAL POSITION
-> CONCRETE JOB CONDITIONS
   {SUBSTANTIVE COMPLEXITY, SUPERVISION, ROUTINIZATION,
    PROTECTIONS/RISKS, TIME/PHYSICAL PRESSURES, INCOME}
-> ACTUAL REQUIREMENTS / OPPORTUNITIES FOR INITIATIVE,
   THOUGHT, JUDGMENT, PROBLEM SOLVING AND CONTROL
-> RECURRENT EXERCISE ACROSS WORK
-> DISTINCT PSYCHOLOGICAL / BEHAVIORAL OUTCOMES
   {IDEATIONAL FLEXIBILITY, SELF-DIRECTED ORIENTATION,
    VALUES, DISTRESS, ALIENATION, LEISURE ACTIVITY}
-> LAGGED PERSON-TO-WORK FEEDBACK WHERE SUPPORTED
-> LATER JOB CONDITIONS
```

The book does **not** directly observe the full middle of every arrow. Its strongest longitudinal evidence identifies reciprocal changes in measured job conditions and psychological functioning; the exact worker action, employer decision, labor-market gate, daily recurrence history, and subjective uptake process are often inferred.

That distinction governs all subsequent M6 products.
