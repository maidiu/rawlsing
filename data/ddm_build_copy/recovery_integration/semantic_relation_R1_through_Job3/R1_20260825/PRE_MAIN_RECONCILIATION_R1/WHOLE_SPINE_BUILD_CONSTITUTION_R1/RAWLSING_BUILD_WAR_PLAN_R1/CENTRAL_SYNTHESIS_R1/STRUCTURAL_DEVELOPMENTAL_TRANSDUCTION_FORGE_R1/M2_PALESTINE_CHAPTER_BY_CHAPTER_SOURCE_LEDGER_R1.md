## Chapter 1 — “Introduction” — Tithi Bhattacharya and Susan Ferguson — pp. 1–6

**PRIMARY QUESTION** — Why use SRT to analyze Palestinian life-making under Israeli settler-colonial capitalism, and how do elimination/exploitation dynamics interact?

Claims about state intent and the total character of the conflict are authorial/editorial claims unless tied to narrower evidence.

**NEGATIVE CEILING** — No direct developmental law; no group membership -> psychology edge; no generic `SETTLER_COLONIALISM -> OUTCOME` operator.

**VALUE TO RAWLSING** — High for multiscale gate/resource hypotheses and historical rebinding; medium as evidence because much is synthesis.

## Chapter 2 — “Gaza: Care, Hope, and Genocide” — Asmaa AbuMezied — pp. 7–30

**PRIMARY QUESTION** — How have Gaza’s formal and informal care structures been constituted, depleted, destroyed, and partially rebuilt?

**EVIDENCE ACTUALLY PRESENT** — Mixed `SECONDARY_SYNTHESIS`, `JOURNALISTIC_REPORT_EVIDENCE`, UN/human-rights `REPORT_EVIDENCE`, some quantitative institutional data, Breaking the Silence testimony, and narratives from the author’s family/friends/social media (p. 7). The chapter is not a designed representative empirical study.

**MECHANISMS RETAINED** — (1) layered care provision across UNRWA, public institutions, households, mutual aid, NGOs, and government (pp. 9–10); (2) power-plant attack/energy shortage -> electricity schedules/fuel shortage -> appliance unavailability -> midnight/manual domestic work and changed care time (p. 11); (3) repeated destruction -> savings diverted to rebuilding -> debt/“minus resources” -> reduced later restoration capacity (p. 12); (4) injury/disability/provider loss -> additional care dependency and reduced care capacity (pp. 12, 14–17); (5) destruction of health infrastructure/medical supplies -> reduced treatment capacity/access (pp. 14–17); (6) displacement/crowded cohabitation -> altered household space, privacy, and relationship demands (pp. 18–21); (7) grassroots food cultivation and seedling production -> locally created food-resource capacity (pp. 23–24); (8) temporary evacuation/opening -> momentary hospital access -> renewed facility loss (p. 26); (9) hunger + family provisioning obligation -> travel to aid gate/site -> exposure to violence (pp. 26–27).

**CLAIMS NOT ADEQUATELY SUPPORTED** — Broad claims of a deliberate strategy to create disability, “hope as a tool of genocide,” general intergenerational transmission of “resistant senses,” and generalized psychological endurance exceed the chapter’s evidence at actor-general causal-law grain. Some are explicit theoretical interpretations.

**NEGATIVE CEILING** — Narratives/social-media material cannot establish population prevalence; political intent is not required for the narrower infrastructure/access/care mechanism and should not be smuggled into it; no direct infrastructure -> psychology or category -> caregiver inference.

**VALUE TO RAWLSING** — Very high for D1+D2 care/infrastructure/cumulative-resource cases; medium for D3; high for evidence-ceiling discipline.

**D1/D2/D3 IMPLICATION** — Strongly strengthens D1 typed resource/time/care pathways and D2 chronic/recurrence/cumulative representation. D3 is supported where collective provisioning actually creates resources, not by symbolic resistance alone.

## Chapter 3 — “Childhood and Social Reproduction in Palestine: They Didn’t Know We Were Seeds” — Mai Abu Moghli and Rachel Rosen — pp. 31–47

**PRIMARY QUESTION** — How are children and the institutions/practices that sustain childhood targeted, disrupted, and reconstituted?

**EVIDENCE ACTUALLY PRESENT** — Predominantly `THEORETICAL_INTERPRETATION` and `SECONDARY_SYNTHESIS`, supplemented by UN/UNRWA, health, detention, and rights-organization reports and some statistics. No original developmental cohort or causal study is presented.

**MECHANISMS RETAINED** — (1) health-system destruction/aid blockage -> prenatal, birth, neonatal, and child-care resource loss (pp. 35–36); (2) school destruction + curfews/checkpoints/incursions -> closure, teacher/student route failure, interrupted class participation (pp. 37–38); (3) detention/house arrest/military-court rules -> incarceration or movement restriction -> interrupted schooling and social contact (pp. 39–40); (4) repeated displacement -> changed location -> fractured school and counterpart continuity (p. 40); (5) children/young people engage in reporting, cooking, farming, and aid activity under war (pp. 44–45), but the downstream structural effect is not established.

**CLAIMS NOT ADEQUATELY SUPPORTED** — `SOCIAL_REPRODUCTICIDE`, `SOCIAL_DEPRODUCTION`, and `UNCHILDING` are theoretical/political analytic labels, not executable causal operators. Assertions that adult inability to protect children necessarily produces communal fracture or that children’s resistance generates developmental outcomes lack direct chapter-level developmental evidence.

**NEGATIVE CEILING** — Structural conditions do not by themselves establish child developmental outcomes. Reports of arrests, closures, casualties, or infrastructure damage establish exposures/conditions, not a uniform psychology.

**VALUE TO RAWLSING** — High for education/access/gate chains and for the developmental firewall.

**D1/D2/D3 IMPLICATION** — D1+D2 cover the strongest evidence. D3 only where an actual organized practice and later social condition are shown; most “resistance” discussion is not sufficient for a structural-feedback law.

**PRIMARY QUESTION** — How do spatial, infrastructural, legal, and reproductive regimes differentially organize “life-making” and “flourishing”?

**EVIDENCE ACTUALLY PRESENT** — Heavy `THEORETICAL_INTERPRETATION` plus `LEGAL_POLICY_EVIDENCE`, UN/report evidence, World Bank research, Human Rights Watch material, and secondary qualitative scholarship.

**MECHANISMS RETAINED** — (1) checkpoint denial/delay -> birth outside hospital access (p. 48); (2) fishing-limit rule -> reachable marine-resource set narrower than resource location -> livelihood/resource loss (p. 57); (3) Military Order 158 -> permit required for water installation -> permits unavailable -> inability to drill/install pumps -> water-access deprivation (p. 57); (4) long-term blockade/food restriction -> nutritional-resource reduction, with reported child stunting outcomes (p. 58; developmental attribution remains evidence-bounded); (5) school-resource/funding inequalities plus travel barriers -> differentiated educational activity conditions (pp. 58–59); (6) license-plate classification + road rules/checkpoints -> route eligibility/delay -> fewer workdays and lower wages in cited World Bank research (p. 59).

**CLAIMS NOT ADEQUATELY SUPPORTED** — `LIVING` vs `FLOURISHING`, “species being,” and claims that checkpoint contact emotionally reproduces every Palestinian as an outsider are theoretical interpretations. Objective gate treatment may be retained without importing the proposed psychology.

**NEGATIVE CEILING** — No direct legal classification -> emotion/identity operator. Macro political characterizations do not replace the concrete rule/position/gate/realization chain.

**VALUE TO RAWLSING** — Very high for D1 gate/access/resource mechanisms and D1+D2 space-time composition.

**D1/D2/D3 IMPLICATION** — Strong D1; D2 needed for delay/uncertainty/ongoing blockade; little direct D3 evidence.

## Chapter 5 — “Decolonialism as Social Reproductive Class Struggle” — Tal-Hi Bitton — pp. 64–87

**PRIMARY QUESTION** — How should capitalist transformation, land/labor relations, and anticolonial struggle be understood through SRT?

**EVIDENCE ACTUALLY PRESENT** — Mainly `THEORETICAL_INTERPRETATION` and `HISTORICAL_EVIDENCE` through secondary histories; no original empirical study.

**MECHANISMS RETAINED** — (1) 1858 Land Code/1878 Land Law -> privatized/title property relation separated from customary use -> indebted cultivators could lose title while still cultivating -> deteriorating conditions and partial wage dependence (pp. 71–72); (2) land acquisition/labor-market organization -> displacement/proletarianization and changed work dependence (pp. 72–74); (3) movement/education/health restrictions -> more resource- and time-intensive reproduction (p. 74), but only at a broad synthesis grain; (4) named organizations historically allocated nursing, food, uniforms, social/cultural work and other roles, demonstrating organization-specific task allocation rather than `GENDER -> CAREGIVER` (pp. 78–79); (5) First Intifada women’s groups/committees built health, education, gardening, neighborhood-defense, and mediation practices (pp. 77–78), a limited collective-resource construction path.

**CLAIMS NOT ADEQUATELY SUPPORTED** — `DECOLONIALISM AS CLASS STRUGGLE`, “protagonism,” struggle-generated self-transformation/confidence/skills, and historical-personality claims are theoretical propositions, not empirically demonstrated person-level laws in this chapter.

**NEGATIVE CEILING** — Do not infer psychological development from participation in struggle; do not treat “imperialism,” “capitalism,” or “settler colonialism” as a one-step causal operator.

**VALUE TO RAWLSING** — High for historical transition/rebinding and organization-specific task allocation; medium for D3.

**D1/D2/D3 IMPLICATION** — D1 must support property/role/labor transformations through explicit institutions; D2 handles before/after historical rebinding; D3 may represent organized alternative provisioning but is not proven as general revolutionary transformation.

## Chapter 6 — “Scholasticide and Social Reproduction in Palestine” — Susan Ferguson — pp. 88–112

**PRIMARY QUESTION** — How have successive governing authorities shaped Palestinian education, and how have Palestinians maintained/recreated schooling under closure and repression?

**EVIDENCE ACTUALLY PRESENT** — `HISTORICAL_EVIDENCE` and `SECONDARY_SYNTHESIS` with UNESCO/World Bank/official statistics, prior qualitative studies, documentary curriculum/textbook research, policy history, and quoted educator/student material. No original field study by Ferguson is presented.

**MECHANISMS RETAINED** — (1) school/campus destruction, book bans, closure orders, denied travel passes/visas, checkpoints, detention -> resource/route/counterpart loss and interrupted schooling (pp. 88–89); (2) First Intifada school closures -> Popular Committees create classrooms in homes/mosques/churches/gardens -> outlawing/raids -> underground continuation (pp. 89, 101–102); (3) prolonged closures/curfews/checkpoints -> lost class time and arduous teacher routes; quoted teacher reports >2-hour mountain travel and severe fatigue (pp. 100–101); (4) 1994 Transfer Agreement -> administrative authority rebinding to PA while some policy/curriculum approval remained externally constrained (pp. 102–103); (5) PA curriculum/budget decisions -> school-content/resource conditions; teacher strikes -> firing/transfers of activists (pp. 104–105); (6) repeated destruction -> rebuilding/online/makeshift education (pp. 107–108), but learning/developmental effectiveness is not established by persistence alone.

**CLAIMS NOT ADEQUATELY SUPPORTED** — Claims that schooling necessarily produces liberation, hope, or resistance; that repression causally “foments resistance” generally; and that present student disillusionment is caused by specific curriculum choices are interpretive and not established as strong causal laws by the chapter evidence.

**NEGATIVE CEILING** — Literacy/enrollment persistence cannot by itself prove which mechanism caused it. Schooling exposure != developmental effect; curriculum content != actor belief.

**VALUE TO RAWLSING** — Very high for D1+D2 and unusually valuable for D3 because the chapter shows action, coordination, external suppression, adaptation, and continued alternative service provision.

**D1/D2/D3 IMPLICATION** — D1 and D2 strongly supported; D3 must allow success, suppression, partial persistence, and a changed practice/network without requiring formal rule change.

## Chapter 7 — “Checkpoints, the Sexual Division of Labour, and Social Reproduction in the West Bank” — Jemima Repo — pp. 113–126

**PRIMARY QUESTION** — How do checkpoints and work-permit regimes extend beyond commuters to reorganize household time, labor, care, and relationships?

**EVIDENCE ACTUALLY PRESENT** — Strongest original `ETHNOGRAPHIC_QUALITATIVE_EVIDENCE` in the volume for the commissioned seam: interviews with married women aged 28–50 in Al-Walajah whose husbands commute through Checkpoint 300 (pp. 120–123), supplemented by OCHA/WHO/ILO/PCBS statistics and extensive prior scholarship. The chapter does not state the interview sample size or full sampling/method details in the text; names are pseudonyms (p. 126 n.27).

**MECHANISMS RETAINED** — (1) movement obstacles/checkpoints -> route/access delay to roads, services, agriculture (pp. 114–115); (2) wall/checkpoint placement + permit difficulty -> agricultural land becomes unreachable -> cultivation can cease -> later property/use vulnerability (p. 115); (3) checkpoint/residency restrictions -> family separation and reduced attendance at births, weddings, funerals, ordinary visits -> counterpart/support ecology change (p. 115); (4) medical permit rule -> application/approval/companion/ambulance gate -> realized access or denial/delay (pp. 115–116); (5) work-pass eligibility requires a man 30–50, married with children, and excludes specified records of protest/union/arrest/dismissal -> actor-specific employment gate (pp. 117–118); (6) work pass/checkpoint schedule -> 2–4 a.m. waking, long queues/commutes, return before expiry -> low household presence (p. 118); (7) linked commuter’s absence -> wife assumes childcare, housework, bills, shopping, in-law care, school/medical accompaniment and duplicated meal preparation -> focal actor time/task ecology change (pp. 120–121); (8) schedule/time poverty -> postponed marital conversation and less father-child interaction (pp. 122–123), with psychological interpretations bounded to interviewee reports; (9) post-Oct-2023 mass permit cancellation/checkpoint closure -> loss of employment access and household income; later restricted reopening/limited worker return after business pressure (pp. 124–126 notes) -> transition/rebinding.

**CLAIMS NOT ADEQUATELY SUPPORTED** — The macro claim that the checkpoint system is designed to reproduce a sexual division of labor is an authorial interpretation beyond the narrower demonstrated gate/time/task-transfer chain. Wives’ reports about husbands’ anger are not direct interviews with the husbands and cannot ground a general psychological law.

**NEGATIVE CEILING** — Do not infer `MAN -> WAGE WORKER` or `WOMAN -> CAREGIVER`; the gate, sector, marital/dependency relation, commute, and actual household allocation must be explicit. No direct child developmental outcome follows from reduced father contact.

**VALUE TO RAWLSING** — Highest-value chapter for D1+D2 and the linked-life seam.

**D1/D2/D3 IMPLICATION** — Requires D1 to support relation-specific linked-actor resource/time/task/obligation transfer and D2 recurrence/schedule semantics. This is a generalization of existing `LINKED_LIFE_TRANSMISSION + HOUSEHOLD_CARE_DEPENDENCY + ROLE_OBLIGATION`, not a new D4.

## Chapter 8 — “Insurgent Social Reproduction: The Home, the Barricade, and Women’s Work in the 1936 Palestinian Revolution” — Mai Taha — pp. 127–153

**PRIMARY QUESTION** — What did household and women’s everyday work concretely do in the 1936–39 revolt, and how did home/market/communication relations change?

**EVIDENCE ACTUALLY PRESENT** — Substantial `HISTORICAL_EVIDENCE` and `ETHNOGRAPHIC_QUALITATIVE_EVIDENCE`: Taha explicitly draws primarily on Faihaa Abdulhadi’s early-2000s oral-history interviews with women who participated in the revolt and the Palestine Oral History Archive, supplemented by literary works, Library of Congress photographs, and official/secondary historical sources (pp. 128–129). Retrospective oral histories impose recall/selection limits.

**MECHANISMS RETAINED** — (1) official raids -> destruction/mixing of food, broken pots/grain bins -> recurrent household cleanup/reconstitution after raids (pp. 136–139); (2) revolutionary organization -> repeated schedules of cooking, collecting food, carrying water, sheltering/hiding people, transporting weapons/ammunition -> recurrent provisioning network (pp. 139–141); (3) household budgeting/resource mobilization -> jewelry sold for weapons, household budget cut, home food production, mutual aid exchange (p. 141); (4) 1938 identity-card/traffic-pass rules + road patrol -> boycott/route avoidance -> women transport goods, village homes expand food production -> temporary market/exchange network (pp. 141–143); (5) women as oral messengers -> safe-route/next-step/weapons information transfer across named villages (p. 143); (6) collective strike/merchant enforcement -> British arrests and sanctions (pp. 144–145); (7) rebel forced-veiling edicts -> women’s protest/refusal, showing internal collective action can reproduce coercive relations even inside anti-colonial struggle (pp. 144–145).

**VISUAL/DOCUMENTARY CORROBORATION** — Figures 8.1–8.3 (pp. 137–138) show official-raid aftermath including broken household objects and grain bins; Figure 8.4 (p. 142) shows fruit baskets described as “smuggled”; Figure 8.5 (p. 143) shows a temporary vegetable market at Bittir station; Figure 8.6 (p. 146) shows a deserted bazaar during the strike. The photographs corroborate material scenes, not the author’s broader causal interpretation or actors’ intent.

**CLAIMS NOT ADEQUATELY SUPPORTED** — “Home as revolutionary space,” emancipation/protagonism, and action-generated transformed consciousness are interpretive conclusions. The chapter itself marks some retrospective claims as remembered/uncertain.

**NEGATIVE CEILING** — Oral history supports reported practices and meanings but not universal prevalence or precise counterfactual causal effect. Alternative provisioning can change a social-resource network without changing the governing rule.

**VALUE TO RAWLSING** — Highest-value historical chapter for D3 and recurrent activity; strong D1+D2 examples.

**D1/D2/D3 IMPLICATION** — D3 survives strongly if it permits temporary resource/network/institution changes, suppression, and partial success rather than requiring formal structural victory. No direct actor-action -> structure edge is licensed.

## Chapter 9 — “‘Genocidal Hauntings of Pronatalism’: The Dialectics of Assisted Reproduction in Israel/Palestine” — Sigrid Vertommen, Weeam Hammoudeh, and Michal Nahman — pp. 154–174

**PRIMARY QUESTION** — How do assisted-reproduction rules, markets, labor chains, imprisonment, and fertility infrastructures organize differential reproductive possibilities?

**EVIDENCE ACTUALLY PRESENT** — Mixed `LEGAL_POLICY_EVIDENCE`, `STATISTICAL_DEMOGRAPHIC_EVIDENCE`, health/human-rights and journalistic reports, and two explicitly identified empirical case studies from the authors’ prior research: Nahman’s transnational Romanian oocyte research and Vertommen/Hammoudeh research on sperm-smuggling by Palestinian prisoners/spouses (p. 163). Nahman interviewed 21 Romanian egg providers (p. 164); the sperm-smuggling discussion uses media accounts and research interviews with women (pp. 167–168).

**MECHANISMS RETAINED** — (1) fertility-clinic destruction -> loss of cryopreserved embryos/gametes and treatment capacity (p. 155); (2) healthcare infrastructure/supply loss -> reduced prenatal/natal/postnatal access and treatment options (pp. 155–157); (3) temporary posthumous-sperm-retrieval order -> surviving partner/parent eligibility without prior deceased consent -> retrieval/reproductive option, with a proposed bill potentially rebinding the rule (pp. 157–158); (4) 2010 egg-donation law’s same-religion requirement + Exception Committee -> actor/couple eligibility constraint; low donor availability compounds realized access (p. 160); (5) domestic egg-donation shutdown after medical misconduct -> Israeli Ministry support for Romanian clinics -> broker/staff recruitment of lower-paid egg providers -> cross-border extraction/fertilization/transport -> Israeli recipient access (pp. 164–165); (6) imprisonment/no conjugal access -> blocked ordinary reproduction -> sperm smuggling + IVF clinic + religious/social acceptance/pro-bono services -> conception/birth for some couples (pp. 166–168); (7) authorities’ refusal to acknowledge paternity -> later legal/status relation remains contested despite birth (p. 167).

**CLAIMS NOT ADEQUATELY SUPPORTED** — The authors explicitly state that “genocidal pronatalism” is **not** intended to mean Israeli state pronatalism is itself genocidal and say they lack ethnographic data for that leap (p. 162). Broader claims about global capitalism requiring demographic elimination are theoretical claims extending beyond the two empirical cases.

**NEGATIVE CEILING** — Fertility technology or religious/national classification does not self-execute; rule, eligibility, provider/clinic, gamete/labor chain, consent/status, and actual procedure must be modeled. Birth does not establish a downstream psychological or political outcome.

**VALUE TO RAWLSING** — Very high for institutional gates, transnational counterpart chains, biological/kinship reproduction decomposition, and D3-like circumvention/alternative route construction.

**D1/D2/D3 IMPLICATION** — Strong D1; D2 for rule changes, imprisonment intervals, specimen transport windows, and altered access; D3 for coordinated circumvention only when the clinic/social/religious infrastructure and external authority response are explicit.

## Whole-volume evidence court

The volume is mechanistically richest when its political/theoretical vocabulary is decomposed into ordinary causal relations:

```text
FORMAL RULE / INFRASTRUCTURE / ORGANIZATIONAL DECISION
-> ACTOR POSITION / ELIGIBILITY / ROUTE / SERVICE CAPACITY
-> RESOURCE / TIME / TASK / COUNTERPART / OBLIGATION ECOLOGY
-> REALIZED ACCESS / DENIAL / RECURRENT PRACTICE / EXCHANGE / EVENT
-> DOWNSTREAM V3 MEDIATION IF SEPARATELY EARNED
```

and, for collective feedback:

```text
REALIZED ACTION
-> COORDINATION / RESOURCE MOBILIZATION / ALTERNATIVE ORGANIZATION
-> EXTERNAL RESPONSE OR COUNTERPART TRANSACTION
-> REALIZED CHANGE (sometimes temporary; sometimes suppressed; sometimes none)
-> LATER ACTOR ECOLOGY
```

The strongest M2 source strata are: Repo’s original household/checkpoint interviews; Taha’s oral-history/archive reconstruction; Vertommen/Hammoudeh/Nahman’s empirical ART cases; and Ferguson’s historically specific education/gate/alternative-provisioning synthesis. AbuMezied, Abu Moghli/Rosen, Bhattacharya, and Bitton contain many useful mechanism candidates, but their strongest Rawlsing use comes after separating report/history evidence from theoretical interpretation and authorial political claims.

The book as a whole does **not** license an undifferentiated `SOCIAL_REPRODUCTION` operator, a category-to-psychology edge, or an action-to-structure shortcut.
