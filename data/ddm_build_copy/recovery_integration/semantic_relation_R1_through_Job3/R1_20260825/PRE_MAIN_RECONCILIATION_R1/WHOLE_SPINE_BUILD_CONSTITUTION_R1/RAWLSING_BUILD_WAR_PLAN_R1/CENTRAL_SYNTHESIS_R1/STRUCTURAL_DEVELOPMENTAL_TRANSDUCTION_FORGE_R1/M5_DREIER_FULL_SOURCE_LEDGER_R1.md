## 1. Claim-status profile

The book combines:

- `THEORETICAL / CONCEPTUAL`: explicit theory of social practice, situated participation, position, perspective, conduct of everyday life, life trajectory, personal practice, stance, learning and change.
- `QUALITATIVE LONGITUDINAL / CASE-BASED`: repeated family-therapy sessions plus interviews following four family members through ordinary contexts over time.
- `PRACTICE-RESEARCH / OBSERVATIONAL-INTERPRETIVE`: analyses of therapy and everyday family, school, work, friendship and mobility practices.

It is not an experimental causal-identification study and does not license universal effect sizes. Its strongest architectural value is mechanism specification, semantic separation and cross-context process tracing.

## 2. Full-book ledger

### Preface; Introduction — pp. xiii–xiv, 1–2

**Inspected content:** Dreier places the project in critical psychology/social-practice theory and states the central displacement: therapy outcomes cannot be understood by inspecting sessions alone because clients live and change across other places and relations.

**Mechanism yield:** expert intervention is only one temporary context in a wider conducted life. Any intervention effect must be realized through later participation elsewhere.

**Rawlsing relevance:** establishes the book-wide anti-shortcut `INTERVENTION_IN_CONTEXT_A != CHANGE_IN_PERSON`; requires a realization path into ordinary contexts.

### Chapter 1 — “Re-Searching Psychotherapy as a Social Practice,” pp. 3–20

**Inspected content:** critique of session-centered process research, professional centeredness, assumptions of identical client functioning in sessions and elsewhere, and attempts to take professional charge of the everyday.

**Mechanism yield:** clients are agents/users who selectively take up, reinterpret, ignore, transform and deploy therapeutic material. Session-produced content does not carry an invariant effect across places.

**Rawlsing relevance:** strongly rejects generic transfer/spillover operators. Supports exact provenance from one context through actor participation to another.

### Chapter 2 — “Theorizing Persons in Structures of Social Practice,” pp. 21–46

**Inspected content:** the core general theory.

#### §2.1 Social practice — pp. 22–28

A social context is a delineated local place in social practice, reproduced and changed through linked participant activities and through connections to other places. Contexts vary in stability, institutionalization, purpose, organization and scope. Material arrangements, routes, technologies, barriers and inter-context links form infrastructures of participation.

A context is therefore **not** synonymous with location, institution, activity, position, relation, rule or social practice as a whole. It is a local structured configuration in which those elements can be related.

Different contexts provide different scopes of possibilities. Power is rendered concretely through unequal scopes of participation and unequal capacities to affect activities/arrangements elsewhere. Structural arrangements distribute positions, rights, obligations, links and routes.

#### §2.2 Persons as situated participants — pp. 28–38

Persons participate from particular locations **and** positions. Location specifies concrete situatedness; position specifies the structured social place, relation and associated scope. The two are related but not interchangeable.

Positions within the same context may give participants different access, authority, obligations, counterpart relations, concerns, perspectives and possibilities. Socially available possibilities become personally usable only in relation to the person’s position, abilities, concerns and actual participation.

Dreier separates contextually available possibility from the person’s understanding/use of it. Persons can reproduce or change practices but always from a partial, positioned grip on them.

#### §2.3 Personal trajectories — pp. 38–46

Persons participate in multiple contexts and move through them over time. They gather stances that help them link, separate, balance and pursue concerns across places. Stances are not reducible to positions or group membership; persons in similar positions can adopt different stances because their broader trajectories and cross-context involvements differ.

Actions in one context can be oriented to consequences in another. Indirect/cross-context relations are therefore part of ordinary personal practice.

**Rawlsing relevance:** strongest primary-source support in this book for D1 and for a multi-context interpretation of D2.

### Chapter 3 — “A Study – Its Design and Conduct,” pp. 47–57

**Inspected content:** study design follows participants through sessions plus interviews about life outside sessions and explicitly treats interviews/research as relations embedded in the ongoing therapy and everyday practice.

**Mechanism yield:** evidence about persons must bind reports/observations to the contexts and positions from which they arise. A person cannot be represented by one decontextualized sample of conduct.

**Rawlsing relevance:** provenance requirement for actor facts; supports context/position/time binding on evidence and realized-history records.

### Chapter 4 — “Clients’ Ordinary Lives Plus Sessions,” pp. 58–83

**Inspected content:** getting outside help, having problems, orientations toward therapy, participation in sessions, diverse perspectives/stakes and later reinterpretations.

**Mechanism yield:** therapy sessions are an additional temporary institutional context with asymmetric roles. Family members occupy different positions and have different stakes even while co-present. The same nominal role has different practical significance across contexts. Session meanings are reinterpreted elsewhere.

**Rawlsing relevance:** `CO-PRESENCE != SAME_POSITION`; `SAME_PERSON != SAME_MODE_OF_PARTICIPATION`; `FORMAL_ROLE != ACTION`; `SESSION_CONTENT != DOWNSTREAM_UPTAKE`.

### Chapter 5 — “Therapy in Clients’ Social Practice across Places,” pp. 84–108

**Inspected content:** diverse modes of participation, distinctive session arrangements, uses of sessions at home, pursuit of concerns and stances across time/place, and experience/reflection/talk across contexts.

**Mechanism yield:** session material becomes usable elsewhere only if it fits the scope of the later context and the person can realize it there. It combines with other circumstances and is used selectively. What is talk in therapy may have to become action, silence, timing, negotiation or another form of participation elsewhere.

Dreier replaces a simple transfer model with **pursuits** that extend discontinuously across occasions/contexts and are reconfigured as circumstances change. Cross-context comparison itself can generate learning.

**Rawlsing relevance:** direct support for typed `CONTEXT_A_CHANGE -> RESOURCE/TIME/OBLIGATION/RELATION/STANCE CHANGE -> CHANGED POSSIBILITY OR ACTIVITY_IN_CONTEXT_B`; rejects an untyped `SPILLOVER` edge.

### Chapter 6 — “Changes in Clients’ Practice across Places,” pp. 109–140

**Inspected content:** personal change, changing from different angles, continuing pursuits/conflicts, judgments of enough change, and changed understandings of change.

**Mechanism yield:** Dreier distinguishes (a) changed participation in a situation, (b) changed abilities/understanding to keep up with a changing situation, and (c) changed abilities/understanding used to change the situation. Intended change may fail or reverse because coparticipants and arrangements matter.

Critically, he distinguishes **variation**, **shift**, and **change**: recurrent or periodic differences across situations/periods need not imply a changed person or changed structure. Stable conduct also requires ongoing accomplishment.

**Rawlsing relevance:** strong D2 support. Recurrence and periodic variation must not be collapsed into transition/change. Strong D3 support because personal action changes social conditions only through actual joint/external processes.

### Chapter 7 — “Changing Problems across Places,” pp. 141–180

**Inspected content:** problem courses, contextual variation, flare-ups, sequences and reconsideration.

**Mechanism yield:** a named problem is not a fixed internal object. Difficulties are embedded in particular activity/relational configurations, can vary across places, and can cease to be problems when possibilities, relations or abilities change. Problems from one context may be acted out or worked over in another, but the route must be traced.

**Rawlsing relevance:** rejects diagnosis/label as a context-free causal state. Supports cross-domain propagation only with exact relational/activity mediators.

### Chapter 8 — “The Conduct of Everyday Life and the Life Trajectory,” pp. 181–197

#### §8.1 Conduct of everyday life — pp. 181–189

**Inspected content:** the central cross-context mechanism. Persons must compose participation across different contexts by organizing tasks, time, routines, relationships, concerns and commitments. They link, separate, balance and sequence activities under arrangements they do not fully control. Conduct is practical and socially situated, not a private master plan.

Routines have daily/weekly/other rhythms and can economize ordinary coordination while carrying familiarity and personal significance. Cross-context comparison creates learning possibilities. The stability/consistency of personal functioning may partly depend on the stability of the conducted arrangement of life rather than an unchanging internal trait.

#### §8.2 Life trajectory — pp. 189–197

**Inspected content:** life trajectory is explicitly criticized when treated as a process only in time. It stretches across **times and places**. Institutions arrange participation sequences, durations, entry/exit criteria and expected successors, especially for children and youth. But personal trajectories are not identical to institutional trajectories.

Dreier rejects simple naturalized stage models where sociocultural transitions are mistaken for universal internal developmental stages.

**Rawlsing relevance:** strongest book-wide support for simultaneous multi-context participation and D2. Trajectory cannot be only a timestamped event list; neither should it become a free-standing causal substance.

### Chapter 9 — “The Children’s Changing Conducts of Everyday Life and Life Trajectories,” pp. 198–225

**Inspected content:** Angie and Donna.

**Mechanism yield:** Angie’s mobility, friends, riding, school and family life show how new possibilities in one context can motivate acquisition of mobility/transport abilities and alter participation in others. Her change is triggered substantially outside the family even when parents interpret home change as causal. Weekend/seasonal contexts can reorganize the significance of the rest of the week.

Donna’s school/friend/family involvements show separated “worlds,” differentiated participation, school transitions and trust/relational consequences. A transition can rebind possibilities without immediately becoming a turning point.

**Rawlsing relevance:** concrete D1+D2 examples; strong evidence against single-context person models and against causal attribution from the observer’s preferred context.

### Chapter 10 — “The Parents’ Changing Conducts of Everyday Life and Life Trajectories,” pp. 226–254

**Inspected content:** Mary and Paul.

**Mechanism yield:** paid work, family care, household organization, schedules, personal pursuits and changing children’s trajectories constrain and enable one another. Mary’s work/care decisions and Paul’s work/family participation show that nominal positions such as mother/father/worker do not specify actual activity, time exposure or influence.

Changes in linked persons’ trajectories reconfigure one another’s time, obligations and possibilities.

**Rawlsing relevance:** `LINKED_LIFE_TRANSMISSION` must be typed through dependence, obligation, schedule, resource and relation changes—not by generic family membership.

### Chapter 11 — “The Changing Conduct of Everyday Family Life and Family Trajectory,” pp. 255–286

**Inspected content:** family conduct and family trajectory.

**Mechanism yield:** family life is an overlapping but non-identical set of members’ conducts, embedded in work, school, transport, neighborhood, shopping and other contexts. The family is not a psychological super-agent. Joint life requires ongoing synchronization of times, presence/absence, tasks, obligations and relations, with both routines and flexible adjustment to passing opportunities.

Changes in one member may require changed arrangements and actions by others. Parents can change permissions, care, transport, task distributions and other arrangements, altering later possibilities. This is an empirical-theoretical model of reciprocal social-practice update.

**Rawlsing relevance:** strongest D3 material in the book and decisive evidence for `ONE_PERSON + MULTIPLE_CONTEXTS` as ordinary rather than exceptional.

### Chapter 12 — “Research in Social Practice,” pp. 287–314

**Inspected content:** researching persons in practice, conceptual/empirical outcomes and uses of research.

**Mechanism yield:** researchers themselves occupy positions and partial perspectives. General concepts must preserve the changing configurations, relations and situated links through which effects occur. A causal variable should not be treated as a fixed invariant entity when the process itself changes its composition and significance.

**Rawlsing relevance:** supports provenance, context/time rebinding and rejection of a generic `CONTEXT`, `POWER`, `AGENCY`, `TRAJECTORY` or `SPILLOVER` black box.
