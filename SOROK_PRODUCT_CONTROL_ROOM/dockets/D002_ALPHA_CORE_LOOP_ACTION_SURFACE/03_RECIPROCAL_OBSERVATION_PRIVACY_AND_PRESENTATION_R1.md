# D002 — Reciprocal Observation, Privacy and Presentation R1

Status: `PROVISIONAL_PRODUCT_LAW_INPUT__NOT_YET_DOCKET_DECISION`

## 1. Core theorem

**The observer is observable.**

ATTEND is not an invisible camera.

Where the user stands, where their eyes move, how long they look, what they touch, what they ask, whether their behavior matches their stated interest and whether they attempt to hide attention can itself be perceived and interpreted by other actors.

## 2. Information acquisition has a social visibility profile

The same desired information can be acquired in materially different ways:

- incidental exposure;
- quick glance;
- prolonged inspection;
- open question;
- covert observation;
- handling an object;
- searching;
- asking a third party;
- waiting for voluntary disclosure.

These are not socially or ethically equivalent.

## 3. User presentation is an attempt, not an achieved fact

The user may specify:

> I ask about the Rome magnet, pretending that is all I am interested in, while sneaking a glance at the bill.

Rawlsing may record:
- overt speech target: magnet;
- intended presentation: casual interest;
- covert attentional target: bill;
- attempted concealment.

The user does **not** control:
- whether Nadia sees the glance;
- whether she notices the divided attention;
- whether she believes the cover;
- whether she cares;
- what she infers.

General law:

> **The player authors the performance; other actors author whether the performance works on them.**

This applies to:
- politeness;
- lying;
- flirting;
- bluffing;
- pretending not to care;
- attempted sincerity;
- apology;
- covert curiosity.

## 4. Reciprocal inference

While the user models Nadia, Nadia may be modeling the user.

This enables:
- correct detection;
- missed detection;
- false suspicion;
- false reassurance;
- meta-misunderstanding.

Example:
- user covertly glances at a bill;
- Nadia does not actually see the glance;
- Nadia nevertheless thinks the user saw the overdue notice because of where they were standing;
- Nadia becomes embarrassed;
- user notices her changed behavior and concludes she caught them snooping.

Neither model need match objective history.

## 5. Distributed epistemic objects

The interaction architecture must preserve separately where relevant:
- objective event;
- user perception;
- Nadia perception;
- user's model of Nadia;
- Nadia's model of user;
- user's model of Nadia's model of user;
- Nadia's model of user's model of Nadia.

Do not instantiate every meta-model continuously if unnecessary, but the ontology must allow the cases where they matter.

## 6. Privacy is not a UI label

Objects and topics should not be marked:
- `PRIVATE`;
- `SAFE`;
- `RELATIONSHIP LEVEL 4 REQUIRED`.

Privacy is socially and personally situated.

A fridge bill, medicine cabinet, family photograph, handwritten religious text, child's homework or phone screen may have very different meanings for different people.

The user must infer:
- what is physically accessible;
- what is socially permitted;
- what is relationally appropriate;
- what they are willing to risk knowing.

Physical accessibility does not equal relational permission.

## 7. Narrator law

The narrator may report externally observable user behavior and acquired information.

Example:

> You ask about the Rome magnet and glance down. The bill is partly covered by the school menu. You catch “PAST DUE” in red before Nadia steps closer to the refrigerator and removes the magnet.

The narrator must not add:

> Nadia noticed you snooping.

unless the user's phenomenal evidence supports that fact.

Even Nadia covering the bill is not automatic proof of motive.

The user receives action, not omniscient interpretation.

## 8. Social context changes information cost

Inspecting the same fridge while:
- Nadia stands beside the user;
- Nadia is in another room;
- Nadia is in the shower;
- Nadia is at work;
- the child is present;
- another guest is present;

creates different:
- detection probability;
- social meaning;
- privacy implications;
- available time;
- possible interruptions;
- downstream interpretations.

The object does not carry one universal inspection consequence.

## 9. Curiosity is relational

The game should not reduce curiosity to “find more lore.”

A player may have to ask:
- Do I need to know this?
- Can I ask?
- Is covert acquisition worth what it means to acquire it covertly?
- Am I attending to this person, or surveilling them?
- Is my curiosity directed toward their needs or toward my own reassurance?

The system does not lecture the user with those questions in the moment.

It makes the questions materially real through consequence.

## 10. Player-praxis implication

Across sufficient history, attention behavior may reveal patterns such as:
- disproportionate monitoring of others' evaluation of the user;
- privacy-boundary testing;
- preference for covert over direct information;
- rapid self-referential inference under ambiguity;
- practical versus symbolic attention;
- willingness to tolerate not knowing.

These are observational hypotheses, not diagnoses.
