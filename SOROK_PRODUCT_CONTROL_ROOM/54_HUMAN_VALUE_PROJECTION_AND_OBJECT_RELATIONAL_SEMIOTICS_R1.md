# Sorok — Human-Value Projection and Object Relational Semiotics R1

Status: PROVISIONAL_PRODUCT_MECHANISM

Date: 2026-10-01

## 0. Holding

Within Rawlsing's relational-materialist design, objects should not receive relational meaning from a universal symbol dictionary.

A persona develops ways of investing human value into material objects through labor, use, care, memory, scarcity, exchange, social recognition, projects, gifts, obligations, loss and histories with other people.

The concrete object therefore has at least three noncollapsed layers:

1. MATERIAL / WORLD OBJECT
2. ACTOR-OBJECT VALUE RELATION
3. COUNTERPART-SPECIFIC RELATIONAL SIGNIFICATION OF SHARING / WITHHOLDING / USING THAT OBJECT

OBJECT != VALUE RELATION != RELATIONAL SIGN TO A PARTICULAR OTHER.

## 1. Actor-object value relation

Candidate generative inputs include:

- material use and embodied affordance;
- labor, maintenance and care invested;
- acquisition history and provenance;
- who made, gave, bought, repaired or used it;
- memories and life periods condensed around it;
- connection to projects, strivings and survival;
- scarcity, replaceability and material security;
- exchange value and commodity mediation;
- status, recognition and social distinction;
- obligation, debt and reciprocity;
- ritual, taboo, inheritance or nonexchangeability;
- histories of loss, deprivation, dispossession or abundance;
- current network relations attached to the object.

This produces an actor-local object-value state. It is not an intrinsic property of the object.

## 2. Relational materialism

The governing design principle is:

VALUE-BEARING OBJECTS ARE MATERIAL NODES IN HUMAN RELATIONS.

A persona may experience an object as bearing the sediment of:
- another person's labor;
- care received;
- care given;
- a promise;
- recognition;
- humiliation;
- sacrifice;
- survival;
- status;
- belonging;
- exclusion;
- dependence;
- shared history.

The object's significance can therefore be real and causally powerful without being treated as an inherent mystical essence.

## 3. Commodity / alienation / fetishism distinction

Do not model "fetishized" as a personality trait or simple false-belief score.

Commodity mediation can make social relations appear in object/exchange form. A persona may, in different domains and moments:

- consciously recognize an object's significance as human and relational;
- experience price, ownership, scarcity, prestige or exchangeability as if they exhaust its value;
- use an object to mediate recognition by others;
- resist commodification of an object whose meaning depends on a human relation;
- simultaneously know an object's relational provenance and care intensely about its market value.

These are domain- and history-specific modes, not one global moral axis.

## 4. Differential sharing operator

The same object should have different relational meaning when shared with different others.

Runtime composition:

ACTOR_OBJECT_VALUE_STATE
+ PERSON_RELATIONAL_GRAMMAR
+ COUNTERPART / ROLE
+ MODEL_OF_THIS_OTHER
+ DYADIC_HISTORY_AND_CONVENTIONS
+ MODE_OF_TRANSFER_OR_ACCESS
+ CURRENT_CONTEXT
-> COUNTERPART_SPECIFIC_OBJECT_RELATIONAL_MEANING

Modes include:
- show;
- allow to touch/use;
- lend;
- give;
- share jointly;
- entrust;
- ask to repair;
- ask to safeguard;
- sell;
- refuse;
- reclaim;
- discard;
- replace;
- permit alteration.

## 5. Meaning packet

For the actor, the generated packet may contain uncertain propositions such as:

- what sharing this object says about who this other is to me;
- what part of myself/history I am exposing;
- what use by this other would feel ordinary, intimate, risky, degrading, generous, controlling or sacred;
- what obligations or reciprocity I expect;
- what I expect the other to understand without explanation;
- what misuse, refusal, loss or replacement would mean;
- whether giving changes the object's meaning or the relation itself;
- whether money/exchange would violate, preserve or transform its meaning.

These are actor-local propositions, not objective relationship truth.

## 6. Example: book

The same book may be:

- cheap and materially replaceable;
- heavily annotated through years of study;
- associated with a deceased parent;
- part of the actor's intellectual self-formation;
- normally lent freely to acquaintances.

Lending it to:
- a child may signify initiation or hope;
- a peer may signify intellectual recognition;
- a lover may signify self-exposure;
- a coworker may be ordinary practical sharing;
- an authority figure may be strategic self-presentation;
- a person who previously lost possessions may feel risky despite affection.

The object alone does not determine the meaning.

## 7. Example: fridge

"Help yourself to the fridge" can carry material and relational layers:

- food is ordinarily communal in this household;
- scarcity makes unrestricted access materially consequential;
- feeding others is a primary care language for this actor;
- accepting food may signify belonging rather than debt;
- with another person, taking without asking may instead signify entitlement.

Thus a standing access permission is simultaneously:
- a concrete world affordance;
- a material-resource relation;
- a possible relational sign.

These layers must not collapse.

## 8. Transfer changes value

Giving, lending, sharing, losing, damaging or selling an object can change the actor-object value relation.

A gift can:
- move an object into a new relational history;
- intensify the giver's tie to the receiver;
- create expected reciprocity or explicitly renounce it;
- convert private memory into shared memory;
- feel like alienation, liberation, sacrifice, recognition or ordinary practicality.

The recipient's meaning may diverge from the giver's.

## 9. No universal hierarchy

Do not infer that:
- more expensive object = more relational value;
- heirloom = always intimate;
- private object = always more meaningful;
- giving = closer than lending;
- sharing food = universal kinship;
- refusal to share = low trust.

Meaning is generated from material history, actor history and counterpart-specific relation.
