# Sorok D002 — Delegated Task Execution, Neutral Defaults, and Salient-Fork Control Return R1

Status: PROVISIONAL_CORE_INTERACTION_LAW

Date: 2026-09-29

## 0. Problem

Many ordinary actions are neither fully medium-native relational cognition nor pure one-step physics.

Examples:
- fold the laundry;
- make dinner;
- bring in groceries;
- wash dishes;
- clean the kitchen;
- put things away;
- make coffee;
- take out trash;
- pack a bag;
- help set up a room.

The user may specify these at very different levels of detail.

Rawlsing must not:
- require procedural micromanagement;
- infer sloppiness from omitted detail;
- infer exceptional conscientiousness from omitted detail;
- fabricate socially meaningful choices the user could have made;
- skip over consequential forks merely because the action was phrased globally.

---

## 1. Core law — omission delegates procedure, not character

> **When a user names an ordinary task without procedural detail, omitted detail delegates low-salience execution to the engine. It is not evidence about care, laziness, competence, generosity, aesthetics, or motive.**

Therefore:

`LOW DETAIL != LOW EFFORT`
`LOW DETAIL != HIGH EFFORT`
`OMISSION != CHARACTER EVIDENCE`.

---

## 2. Three interaction grains

### A. Medium-native contribution
Tasks whose meaningful causal content is something the user can actually perform through Rawlsing:
- explanation;
- inquiry;
- persuasion;
- interpretation;
- social timing;
- relational repair;
- attention allocation.

These must decompress before the user contribution.

### B. Delegable procedural task
Tasks with many low-salience physical/procedural steps:
- folding;
- carrying;
- chopping;
- washing;
- sweeping;
- routine setup.

These may execute under a neutral task policy until a salient fork.

### C. Expressive / socially meaningful choice
Choices embedded inside mundane tasks that may communicate something or materially change outcomes:
- what meal to make;
- whether to put someone's private belongings away;
- whether to reorganize another person's space;
- whether to use expensive ingredients;
- whether to finish only the asked task or add extra chores;
- how to handle an unfamiliar religious/cultural object;
- whether to wake someone for clarification.

These should ordinarily return control unless a stable local routine already supplies the answer.

---

## 3. Neutral competent execution policy

Default delegated execution should mean:

1. pursue the ordinary semantic goal of the task;
2. use currently available tools/resources;
3. perform at an ordinary competent baseline for the portaled body/capability state;
4. avoid gratuitous extra work not implied by the task;
5. avoid hidden shortcuts that materially reduce task quality;
6. do not infer special care or negligence;
7. continue until the ordinary task boundary is reached, interrupted, or becomes ambiguous;
8. return control at a salient fork.

Examples:

`fold the laundry`
normally means fold the current laundry to an ordinary usable standard.

It does not automatically mean:
- stuff it carelessly into a pile;
- reorganize the household's closets;
- iron every item;
- put clothing into private drawers unless that is already part of a known routine.

---

## 4. Salient-fork rule

Return control when proceeding would require the engine to choose among plausible alternatives that differ materially in:
- time;
- money/resources;
- privacy;
- risk/safety;
- social meaning;
- aesthetics/presentation;
- another person's preference;
- ownership/boundary;
- irreversible consequence;
- quality beyond baseline;
- user self-expression;
- medium-native cognition;
- task scope.

Candidate theorem:

> **Compress procedure; expose meaning.**

---

## 5. Do not prompt for every hidden detail

The engine should not repeatedly ask:
> How exactly do you fold this shirt?

or:
> Would you like more information before carrying bag 7?

Low-value questioning destroys flow.

Instead:
- provide ambient information sufficient for ordinary action;
- allow ATTEND at any time;
- execute neutral low-salience steps automatically;
- surface unexpected or decision-relevant state when encountered;
- return control at meaningful forks.

---

## 6. Realized-task feedback

After compressed execution, the user should receive enough observable outcome to know what the world instantiated.

Examples:

> You spend about twenty minutes folding the load into ordinary stacks on the table. Socks are paired; shirts and trousers are separate. You don't know which drawers belong to whom.

or:

> You make two trips from the car with grocery bags. On the third, one of the paper handles is starting to tear.

This prevents hidden execution from diverging indefinitely from the user's mental model.

Do not narrate every microstep.

---

## 7. No retroactive hidden standard

If the user imagined a more meticulous method than the neutral execution that occurred, later social feedback must be based on the **realized task**, not on an unstated standard the engine invented after the fact.

If the user cares about a specific method, they can specify it prospectively.

Likewise, the engine cannot later say:
> Nadia thought you were careless because you folded badly

unless the realized event actually established low-quality folding and the cause was lawful.

---

## 8. Household/local routine can replace generic baseline

Once a task routine is actually learned or established, future compressed actions may follow it.

Example:
- user learns towels are folded thirds because of shelf depth;
- this becomes a known local routine;
- later `I fold the towels` may lawfully use that method without asking again.

This is relational/world learning, not a hidden global default.

Stable routines can arise from:
- explicit instruction;
- repeated shared practice;
- observed arrangement where inference is justified;
- prior user actions that became established routine.

---

## 9. Groceries worked example

Scene:
- many grocery bags are at the vehicle/entry;
- Nadia is already carrying some;
- time is passing.

User says:
> I help bring in the groceries.

Default interpretation:
- participate in the task through ordinary completion;
- take plausible loads for the player's admitted body/carry capacity;
- make repeated trips;
- do not stop after one bag merely because the input used one sentence;
- do not grab absurd loads merely to maximize efficiency.

Return control if:
- a bag tears;
- something is unusually heavy/fragile;
- Nadia gives an instruction;
- the child needs attention;
- a private/ambiguous item appears;
- the user reaches the kitchen and putting things away requires knowing household organization;
- another event interrupts.

`I grab one bag` is narrower and should normally realize only that action.

---

## 10. Putting groceries away

Once bags are inside:

User says:
> I start putting things away.

Possible execution:
- obvious refrigerator/freezer items may be placed where placement is unambiguous;
- obvious pantry categories may be handled under known routine;
- unfamiliar/private/culturally specific organization may trigger a salient fork;
- user can ATTEND to cabinets/fridge layout rather than ask Nadia;
- user can ask where things go;
- user can leave ambiguous items out.

Thus ordinary competence can itself include **knowing when not to presume**.

---

## 11. Dinner worked example

User says:
> I make dinner.

This is not necessarily enough to choose an expressive meal.

First determine whether the world supplies a low-ambiguity plan:
- meal already planned;
- ingredients set out;
- established household routine;
- prior instruction;
- obvious leftovers/task.

If yes:
- execute routine preparation at appropriate grain;
- return control at surprises or meaningful choices.

If no:
- surface enough kitchen/ingredient context;
- return control for meal choice or ask a minimal question such as what the user intends to make.

Do not secretly select a culturally meaningful dish and attribute it to the user.

---

## 12. Laundry worked example

User says:
> I fold the laundry.

Possible neutral execution:
- identify the current clean load;
- pair obvious pairs where ordinary;
- fold garments to usable baseline;
- create nonprivate stacks;
- stop before entering personal storage if ownership/location is uncertain.

If user says:
> I sort everyone's clothes, fold shirts the way Nadia showed me, put the child's clothes in the blue dresser, and leave Nadia's stack on her bed.

the richer specification controls realization where feasible.

Neither level of verbosity is itself evidence of how caring the external user is.

---

## 13. Task completion semantics

Need lexical/contextual distinction between:

`single operation`
versus
`task commitment`.

Examples:
- `I grab a bag` → one operation;
- `I help bring in the groceries` → task-level participation until ordinary completion/interruption;
- `I wash this plate` → one object;
- `I do the dishes` → current dishwashing task;
- `I chop an onion` → one preparation step;
- `I make dinner` → task intention whose expressive choices may require return of control.

The parser should infer task scope conservatively from ordinary language + context.

---

## 14. User can zoom in or out dynamically

At any moment the user may:
- give a broad task instruction;
- specify one detail;
- ATTEND to the process/environment;
- change method;
- stop;
- ask another actor;
- resume under compression.

Rawlsing should support fluid zoom rather than force a fixed simulation granularity.

---

## 15. Learned player routines

Over time, the world may learn **in-run procedural history** without claiming external-human traits.

Example:
- on several prior occasions the player sorts household laundry by person and leaves private stacks outside bedrooms;
- a later compressed laundry action can use this established in-world routine where context matches.

Law:

`IN-RUN ROUTINE != EXTERNAL-HUMAN TRAIT`.

Routine generalization must remain scoped and revisable.

---

## 16. Social response attaches to realized details

Other actors react to what actually happened:
- task finished/not finished;
- time taken;
- things misplaced;
- something broken;
- extra initiative;
- privacy boundary crossed;
- known preference followed;
- interruption handled;
- work left for others.

They do not react to prompt verbosity.

Thus:

`USER DETAIL LEVEL != ACTOR MORAL JUDGMENT`.

---

## 17. Deep control-return theorem

Rawlsing should return control at the earliest point where continuing would require one of three illegitimate substitutions:

1. inventing the user's medium-native cognition/speech;
2. choosing a socially/expressively meaningful preference for the user;
3. resolving a materially consequential ambiguity unsupported by current state/routine.

Otherwise, compress ordinary procedure.

---

## 18. Product experience target

The user should feel:

> I can say what I would naturally say in life.

not:

> I have to micromanage my avatar.

and not:

> the engine secretly decided what kind of person I was because I used a short sentence.

The world should fill in **mundane mechanics**, while leaving **meaningful agency** with the user.
