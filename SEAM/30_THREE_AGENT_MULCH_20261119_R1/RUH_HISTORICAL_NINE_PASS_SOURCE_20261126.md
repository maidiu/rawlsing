#### Pressure-flow as the historical production of possible life — CO HQ theory-MULCH, 2026-11-25

**Positive constitutive articulation; proposed author-HOME receiving, not a new authority contract, runtime owner, validated science, or protected accession.** The following develops the meaning of this whole movement, and of Movements II–IV and VI–X, rather than replacing their historically conserved paragraphs. The A–F articulation below survives as a more particular treatment of plural present activity. R50 is an important but narrow realization within that treatment; it is not the measure of the wider conception.

##### 0. Second THEORY-MULCH — the production of the producer, and the power of its products (2026-11-26)

**Controlling interpretive advance within this amendment, not a new HOME.** The November 25 synthesis below corrected hydraulic pressure-flow but remained too close to a moving subject encountering and modifying channels. It spoke well about acquisition and the transformation of relations, yet could be read as though the organism already possessed the movement, its object and the significance of its circumstances. That does not yet explain historical production of desire, alienation, the formation of an unconscious, the origin of a power over oneself, or how a person comes to be organized through the very products of their activity. The following is a positive successor to that residual grammar. Preserve its predecessor as particular discriminations under the stronger conception.

**Pressure-flow is the historical process in which living activity produces, appropriates, reproduces and encounters its own conditions of possibility, including the organization of the living subject, and in which these conditions can become powers over or powers of that subject.** The person is not prior to this history except as a particular embodied organism. The unity of the psyche is not its reduction to a common substance. It is the recursive unity of a life whose products, relations and acquired organs of activity return as conditions of its further movement.

###### 0.1. The real beginning is not a stream of already existing desire

The historical pressure-flow conversation demanded a unity beneath the various psychological surfaces. PRE-A0 rightly moved living conation before encountered pressure. But "conation before pressure" can still be read too abstractly. A cry does not begin as a tiny person's demand for acknowledgment. Hunger is already a bodily condition with a history, but it does not contain a particular social object, an anticipatory relation to the caregiver or a symbolic account of satisfaction. A directional movement can occur before those psychological formations exist. The particular thing toward which it comes to be directed, the way that thing becomes distinguishable, and the kind of satisfaction that matters are achievements of history.

Leontiev's argument in Activity, Consciousness and Personality, in the discussion of motives and needs, is more radical than a distinction between a need and a project. A need initially does not know its object; encountering its object gives the need a concrete organization, and the object can acquire a directing function. Need, initially a prerequisite, can become a product of the activity. Later in the personality discussion he describes the reversal in which the subject initially acts to sustain existence and subsequently sustains existence in order to act. Sève's postscript (pp. 495–497 in the supplied English edition) similarly refuses to treat historically human needs as the first fact of the psychology of personality, and insists that subjective need must be confronted with the objective requirements of developing activity. These formulations transform the meaning of the original Ruach/vitality intuition. Its strength is not the proposal of a universal energy but the insistence that there is a real, dynamically organized activity beneath separately named wants.

The concrete productive movement can be seen in ordinary sound. At first there may be breathing, bodily discomfort, incidental sound, orientation to sound, or the pleasing repetition of a movement. A caregiver's independent response may make a certain distinction available: the child undergoes a difference between making a sound and hearing another sound, between a contingent response and no response, between producing a sound with another and producing it alone. The world has changed because their acts have occurred. The child's sensorimotor organization may change through those acts. What was not initially a particular object of pursuit can gradually become one: repeating the sound, hearing it answered, varying it, sustaining rhythm, participating with someone, exploring what the voice can do. These are not latent rewards waiting to be assigned different strengths. They are potential differentiations of activity and its object, made possible in a particular history. No phonological or motivational adult ontology need be projected back onto the first cry.

The crucial consequence is that an activity can produce the need for its own continuation. A previously unknown object, other person or practice can become an organizing concern of a life. Its absence can subsequently become a privation which was not possible before the relationship existed. This is not simply the increase of a pre-existing desire value. The previously undifferentiated situation now has a new kind of significance for the person. Music can become necessary to someone's life even though no biological deficit was originally a need for music. A particular person can become irreplaceable to someone even though the organism was not born needing that person. A child can come to need the independent presence and activity of a playmate, not just some interchangeable supply of positive stimulation. The historical production of desire is also the historical production of vulnerabilities.

This is a stronger reading of productive desire than either the hydraulic one or a graph of competing motives. But the inverse error is to dissolve biological need into historical meaning. An infant starves without adequate nourishment whether or not it understands the event, wants the available food or has an affectively positive relation to feeding. The biologically necessary and the historically necessary can enter the same activity without collapsing. Nor is every historically produced desire indefinitely plastic, consciously endorsed, or reducible to an earlier material deficit. Real people develop new necessities through the activity of maintaining and enlarging their lives. There is no fixed list of the necessities a developing human can acquire.

###### 0.2. The caregiver does not merely open a channel: a new organ of activity comes into existence

Return to the child's sound, reaching or first movement through space. The caregiver can attend to an incipient activity, distinguish an aspect of it, arrange the world so the movement can succeed, and sustain a temporal space in which the child can repeat it. The caregiver's anticipation of a possible achievement can give the interaction a shape not yet available to the child. That anticipation is not automatically transferred into the child. It is enacted in the adult's own activity: moving the object, slowing down, waiting, demonstrating, supporting the body, adjusting the conditions, or answering the sound. The child acts within that transformed world.

There is a qualitative difference between the child having a new occasion for an old act and the child becoming a different kind of causal participant. Imagine that a supported reach becomes a reach the child can reproduce in a new conjunction. Its prior possibility depended on another person's live assistance. Its future possibility can now depend in part on an organization acquired by the child. A piece of the history of their joint activity has become a power of this particular body. That is an appropriation, not a copy of the caregiver's intention, and not the transfer of a quantity of vitality from one body to another. The child owns the newly acquired way of acting in the sense that its exercise is now an activity of the child's embodied organization, not in the sense that the child produced all of its conditions alone.

Call this, interpretively, a **functional organ of activity**: not a new anatomical organ or a required software object, but an acquired organization by virtue of which a living subject can bring about, distinguish, sustain or transform something it formerly could not. A habit, a language practice, an acquired motor coordination, a distinction between kinds of social claims, an expectation that permits a particular anticipation, or a recurrent way of organizing attention can have this character when it actually operates. The organ may be distributed across embodied and acquired organization, not localized in a single psychological record. Its conditions of exercise may extend outside the organism. Learning to use a tool does not incorporate the tool into the body or make the tool permanently available. Nevertheless the relation between actor and tool is now materially different when the actor has acquired a way of using it. The particular operation is now possible through a new conjunction, not through the arbitrary increase of a generalized ability.

This corrects a weakness of the original desiring-machine metaphor: imagining that the machinic connections are simply internal objects in a person. The child's new organ of activity is a material-historical acquisition; its further exercise still involves real objects and other people. It may become possible with someone else, in another place, or in a novel practice. Generalization itself is an acquired historical power, not an assumption warranted by the name of a skill. Some powers remain context-bound. Some become more transferable. Some cease to be usable after changes in the world or body. Others are recomposed into activities with different objects. A child whose new coordination makes it possible to bring something to another child has not thereby acquired generosity; the new coordination changes which activities can be generated, including helping, playing, exploring and taking. The formation of a capacity and the subsequent production of an object of desire through its exercise are distinct and reciprocally conditioning.

Now distinguish another developmental possibility. A caregiver delights in the child discovering something, not merely in the child directing a particular performance toward the caregiver. The adult's pleasure may become practically productive: patience, renewed material support, an offer of another occasion, willingness to relinquish control. The child can undergo some of this response. A jointly achieved activity can become significant as a way of being with that particular Other. Their recognition may contribute to the child's experience of an effect as socially real, and eventually the child may come to seek the continuation of such activity with the Other. The pleasure of another's developing power may itself become an acquired object of the caregiver's activity. That is one path toward a love in which the Other's capacity to act independently is constitutive of the person's own practical concern.

The content of that relation cannot be established by the caregiver's self-report or the child's smile. It becomes legible in the history of their separate activity: whether the adult can sustain assistance that enlarges the child's possibilities, including when the child turns away from the adult's current wish; whether the adult can relinquish a role without destroying the material support of the child's life; whether the child can acquire a way of continuing without having continually to secure the adult's assent. These are possible functional consequences of a specific kind of relation, not criteria by which the engine certifies love or diagnoses its absence. A caregiver unable to provide assistance because paid work consumes their available hours has not thereby failed to desire the child's flourishing. Material inability, imposed labor and personal orientation must remain distinguishable.

The caregiver may instead become dependent on being the necessary mediator of the child's achievements. The child's acquired power then threatens a practical or historically meaningful organization of the adult's life. What once was the adult's means of enabling the child can become the means of preserving an identity or controlling an opportunity. The same act of assistance, unchanged in external form, can acquire a new function in the relation. The adult might help in one situation and obstruct autonomous exercise in another, without having a uniform motive or a coherent explanation. The child may develop contradictory organizations around the same relation: joy in joint achievement, independent delight in the newly acquired capacity, and inhibition of its exercise where it threatens continued care.

This is not a necessary tragedy of separation. It is the specific possibility that **a power produced through love can come into contradiction with the form of relation through which love had been organized**. Its positive resolution, where it occurs, is not independence from relation but a more differentiated relation between independent powers. A child can become more capable of loving the adult precisely because the adult is no longer the exclusive condition of the child's practical continuation. The adult can become capable of sharing an activity with the child that was not previously possible. Neither is inevitable. The same germ contains both possibility of emancipatory care and possibility of the reproduction of dependency.

###### 0.3. The products of activity confront its producer: objectification, appropriation and alienation

Sève's most consequential corrective to a merely intrapsychic pressure-flow is the reciprocal relation of activity and its products. An activity produces consequences, some of which become conditions of its later production. The accumulated products need not remain at the disposal of the person who produced them. His postscript explicitly distinguishes the functional connection between personality and social relations from mechanical analogy with political economy. Preserve this distinction: a computational psyche is not an economy with conserved units of desire, and the person is not an internally arranged miniature society.

The new organ acquired by the child is one kind of product. A shared plaything, a repaired object, a learned word, a household routine, a reputation, a commitment to another, an institutional rule and a collectively organized resource can be quite different kinds of products. They can become independent conditions of the lives of their producers. A promise is not only a private inclination once the other person has heard it and organized their activity around it. A tool a worker has made can subsequently expand the worker's practical efficacy or remain owned by someone who controls its availability. A practice a caregiver has established can become a continuing material demand on that caregiver, a common means of the household, or an obstacle to the child's later activity. The very activity through which one participates in producing a relationship may reproduce a condition that constrains one's later life.

**Objectification is not alienation.** The fact that something made by a person becomes independently real is what makes it capable of supporting other activities. It need not be a loss of self. Alienation is a possible historically specific relation to such objectified powers and their conditions of appropriation. The person can find that their own developed activity confronts them as something that must be sustained on terms they do not control. One can love doing something, become better at it, and become more dependent through the very growth of one's competence. An employer may appropriate the product of skilled labor while the worker remains unable to determine whether, when or for what purpose the skill is exercised. A child may become extraordinarily competent at maintaining a caregiver's equilibrium, gaining local power over the terms of an interaction at the cost of continually reproducing a relation in which their own other activity is endangered. Neither skill is fictitious merely because it is implicated in domination.

Thus we need a concept of **effective practical power**, distinguished from capacity. Capacity concerns what this organization can perform under qualified conditions. Practical power concerns the actor's actual ability to bring about or sustain consequential activity under the terms of the real relations in which it lives. A person may possess a capacity without having the resources, permission, time, collaborators or ownership needed to exercise it. A previously weak person may participate in changing those terms without becoming individually self-sufficient. An acquired organ can make a person more useful to an institution without making them more capable of directing their own life. An increase in output or technical efficacy is not in itself an increase in freedom.

NEIGHBOR R28 demonstrates a thin but exceptionally valuable germ of this distinction. The visitor produces an actual object, and the actor's prior practice and later optional acquisition can change the efficacy of the actor's work on a subsequent object. The material owner is not thereby the maker; the actor's technical facility is not the actor's ownership of the means or product. The local stock grant constrains what can occur even when skill and intent remain. The exercise of a power produces a thing which enters a shared world under independent material terms. No actual class relation, appropriation psychology, mutual affection, labor exploitation or moral valuation is proved by that fixture. Its value is that the computational distinction has real consequences instead of being an ideological label. A stronger historical conjunction can let a changed condition of use, disposition or dependence alter the future practical significance of an otherwise identical skill. Whether the person subsequently acquires a new desire, interprets its significance or experiences a conflict is a further issue.

The earlier "channel" now acquires a stronger meaning. Its composition contains not only the possibility of activity but the **conditions of its reproduction and of appropriation of its products**. What has to recur for this activity to remain possible? Who must act, at whose expense, with control over what? What is produced which becomes a condition of the next iteration? Who can direct or withhold that product? How does the person's own previous participation enter the current terms? These are concrete questions about living activity and shared world, not abstract scores of power. A groove is not just a route frequently taken. Its reproduction may already be securing its own premise, or undermining it, through the actual results of the actor's activity.

###### 0.4. Pressure is not just a wall in front of activity: it is often the contradictory form of its reproduction

The hydraulic metaphor imagines pressure principally as something blocking or forcing a current. Many of the most consequential pressures do not prevent activity. They make a particular activity continuously necessary. The parent must undertake wage labor in order to continue caring. The same labor may consume the conditions through which the parent could undertake that care. The contradiction is not one between an external pressure and an inner value. It is within the concrete reproduction of the person's life: the means of sustaining one activity can erode its actual possibility, while the person remains dependent on those means. The institution may make the person's ability to act in one register dependent on their reproduction of the institution's terms in another.

Here Holzkamp's distinction between restrictive and generalized action-potence (Handlungsfähigkeit, the capacity to participate in determining one's conditions of life) supplies a serious challenge to a psychology of individual expansion. In Grundlegung der Psychologie, §7.5, particularly the analysis of restrictive agency, self-hostility and active exclusion of reality around pp. 370–383, he investigates how an individual's immediate ability to maintain life under existing power relations can depend on reproducing conditions which restrict it. The possibility of changing those conditions collectively differs categorically from optimizing one's own conduct within them. This is a theoretical orientation, not a universal developmental law or a compulsory emancipatory destination. Do not encode the theorist's account of the true interest of every subject as the subject's actual motive. Nor equate every compromise with self-hostility or every silence with repression.

Consider a caregiver whose continuing material provision for the child requires preserving an employer's approval. This approval need not be pleasurable; it may be an effective condition of obtaining income and avoiding a sanction. Preserving the relationship to the employer can become a means of care, while the employer's control of the caregiver's time diminishes the possibility of care as shared activity. Another person may experience the work as a valued vocation, and the same shift schedule as relief from a difficult home; someone else as a humiliating necessity. The objective contradiction can remain real whether or not the actor thinks of it as one. The caregiver might continue to act within the relation while also desiring to transform it. A different relative's help, a change of employment, publicly provided care, a collectively altered schedule, or a change in the household's allocation of work can make a previously impossible course feasible. The direction of development need not follow a change of attitude. The practical world has changed.

Now the problem of the unconscious and defensive organization can be rooted more deeply. Not every unarticulated cause is a repression. There is a vast history that never became conceptual or focal, things simply not perceived, acquired practical coordination that need not be verbalized, inaccessible memory, and interpretations for which the person has never developed a category. But there are also cases in which the terms under which a person can continue a valued activity make attending to a particular contradiction costly. The person can acquire ways of selectively organizing attention, interpretation, expression and relation which protect continued participation under those terms. A child whose achievement is a condition of tolerated membership may conceal a failure even from their own standing account of what matters. An adult can defend a legitimate good through a practice that undermines that good. Such organization can become persistent and partly inaccessible without being inexplicable or simply irrational. The old flow vocabulary of insulation, buffer and mis-aim must be conserved here as possible differentiated functional relations, not compulsory defense channels or universal latent moral failure.

A stronger functional realization would not merely detect avoidance in response to a threatening cue. It could preserve the actor's actual dependence, the concrete conduct through which this is maintained, the independently real consequence for a second activity, and the history of acquired ways in which that consequence becomes differently receivable or expressible. A change in the real conditions of continuation can make an earlier strategy unnecessary, without guaranteeing that the acquired organization immediately dissolves. An acquired defensive practice can outlive the conditions in which it once made practical sense. Conversely the actor can recognize the contradiction perfectly and remain unable to alter the material terms. Consciousness is an operative moment of activity, not a sovereign switch which releases the subject from its world.

The August structural-pressure formulation correctly insists that determination is not transparency. The stronger proposition is that structural power often **organizes the reproduction of the activity through which the subject itself becomes able to act**, and can thereby operate through the person's own initiative. A constraint external to the psyche can become a constitutive condition of the formation of an active organization without being stored as an internal representation of structure. An institution can generate skilled, enthusiastic participants through practices which reproduce its control. Productive power and domination are not opposite poles on a scalar. A practice can produce real life and simultaneously appropriate it.

###### 0.5. The whole person is organized through the use of their life, not the sum of their experiences

The preceding synthesis distinguished momentary effects, durable changes and slow gravity. That is essential but insufficient if temporality is only a timestamp on records, a scheduler of episodes or a variable determining update frequency. Sève's discussion of use-time in the supplied edition, chapter IV section (2), pp. 331 onward, distinguishes the manifest timetable from the real organization of the time of a life, and situates that organization within the production and exercise of capacities. Dreier's Psychotherapy in Everyday Life (pp. 41–42, section on distributing, linking and balancing participation and stakes) gives a precise complementary account: the significance of someone's local participation depends on its place in their trajectory across contexts, and the same context is a different part of different participants' lives. This is not simply a theory of context switching. It specifies a genuine source of the personal sense of activity.

Return to the child who discovers a practice through coactivity. It will not become an effective constituent of the whole life merely because a developmental record has been admitted. Is there time for its exercise? Is the activity repeatable without the original caregiver? Is it something which the child can carry into a different context, or does the family organization permit it only during a particular interval? Can the child develop related discriminations or capacities? Does the new practice attract time and attention formerly used for something else? Does its exercise produce fatigue, materials, companionship, future opportunity, protection, or obligations? The answer to these questions may itself transform the significance of the practice. These are not after-the-fact external constraints on an internally complete project. The shape of the person's time participates in the formation of the project and the kinds of powers the person has.

A person may work at one place in order to continue something valued elsewhere. Another participant in the same workplace, undertaking the same task, may be developing a power they expect to exercise professionally, or the work may be the only place where their competence is acknowledged. Their different trajectories produce different personal senses of materially similar acts. No one context is the whole person. Yet the contexts are not simply compartments in a personality graph. Their relations can be reproduced by the very movement between them. A person earns money in one activity, purchases material means in another, learns a practice in a third, sustains an important relation in a fourth, and brings an acquired way of responding from one into another. Some relations remain separated because bringing them into contact carries a material or psychological cost. Some are connected through an actual obligation, object, practice or future project. The constitution is organized through these crossings, including crossings which the person does not represent consciously.

This supplies a materially grounded reinterpretation of the original exercise–parental approval example. Exercise can be transformative not because one event hits the approval subgraph with a larger confidence coefficient but because a regular practice reorganizes an actual portion of the person's life. It may offer a continuity, achievement, setting, relationship, or experience of one's own practical agency that is not dependent on the parent's current judgment. The person can be materially engaged in another life-producing relation when they encounter the parent. They may now organize their availability, interpretation or practical response differently. That change can in turn affect the material and emotional conditions under which they continue exercising. The very thing they acquire through activity can alter the real reproduction of the old relation. It may also be captured by the old relation and become a new obligation to sustain it. Which happens cannot be read from the practice's label.

This is where ordinary-life hypercompression becomes more than engineering economy. Compression must conserve how activities make one another possible or impossible, the temporal claims of independent Others, the production and erosion of powers, and the distribution of actual resource and practice opportunities over time. The same hours of "exercise" do not necessarily form the same person when the availability of the practice depends on a parent, when the person is paid to perform it, when it is undertaken privately for enjoyment or when the material conditions make continued practice exhausting. Those differences need not all receive focal scenes; aggregate modeling with explicitly bounded ancestry can conserve some of them. A compressed period cannot legitimately invent remembered interactions or capacities unsupported by its modeled process. Conversely an empty event list is not evidence of inactivity. Qualified coarse realization of regular practical life can be formative without narrative recollection.

The slow gravity of a person can now be understood as a possible effect of the sustained reproduction of a configuration of activity, not merely a high-weight recurrent pattern of internal reactions. A motif becomes organizationally powerful when its constituent practices, obligations, relationships and acquired means repeatedly reproduce conditions for its further recruitment. A person's way of being may remain stable because the world continues to call upon and sustain those practices; it may remain stable under changed conditions because acquired organizations persist; it may transform through a new sustained activity whose own reproduction changes the relations among existing powers. Recurrent motifs and compartments still belong in the PRF, but must answer to the real use of this life. No compulsory attractor, personality label or global motivational hierarchy follows.

###### 0.6. Personal sense can be made and remade by an event's afterlife

Leontiev's bitter-candy example is more radical than the slogan "multiple motives can be active." In the experiment, a child achieved the assigned material result by departing from the adult's stipulation. Only on the adult's return, with the offered reward, did the contradiction become manifest in the child's refusal and tears. The two activities did not necessarily appear as a conflict when the child acted; the subsequently offered reward acquired a different personal sense through their relation. The example is neither a law of shame nor an infant experiment, and we should not infer that every participant would experience it similarly. It shows why we must not demand that the decisive organization of an event's significance be available before the event takes place.

An actual consequence can disclose a relation between activities that had not yet become salient. The same object can be valued before an act, aversive in one conjunction afterwards, and differently significant again through a further historical development. What matters is not a scalar that changed sign, but the changing relation of an object to activities and their actual results. A child can accomplish something and discover that the caregiver is upset by the very achievement; the achievement need not cease to be desired. The caregiver may publicly praise the child while their practical treatment subsequently restricts the new capacity. The child can undergo some of this contradiction without possessing an adult explanation. There can be affective organization before articulated self-knowledge; affect need not be truthful diagnosis of the objective relation.

This transforms PFF's four depths and the temporal afterlife of the actor. The present cannot be adequately computed as a single expression of an already constituted iceberg. The actual event, including the actor's own performed expression and the independent Other's response, can become a condition of a new distinction within the actor. The meaning of previous activity can subsequently be reorganized while the actual prior occurrence remains unchanged. Some transformations require prolonged activity or encounters that make a contradiction materially consequential. Some never occur. A narrator that invents the appropriate insight after the event is not sufficient: the newly acquired significance must become capable of organizing a subsequent operation, even if only an alternative the actor can prospectively recognize and later reject.

PFF's important non-durable carryover is also not merely a store of transient intensities. A current joint activity can leave a practically operative but uncrystallized relation: the actor expects continuation, attends differently, retains an unfinished motion or avoids interrupting another person. These need not already be enduring projects. At the next moment, that organization can become effective, fail to be fulfilled, or dissipate. A later enacted course may qualify historical acquisition of something that could not have been inferred from the first event alone. The early V3 vessel, crack, sublimation and metabolization articulations thereby survive as questions about historically transformed functions and objects, not a menu of diagnostic response types. What appears now as pleasure, embarrassment, hurt or attraction cannot by itself identify the historical cause.

###### 0.7. Love and recognition are not merely relations to an audience: the Other's becoming can become an object of one's own activity

A deeper reading of the caregiver germ must not make the caregiver and infant into reward-seeking entities whose goals happen to align. Consider an adult whose concern becomes the child's increasing capacity to live, and whose own life is affected by the real expansion of the child's powers. The adult's activity is not reducible to self-satisfaction even if it is pleasurable. Pleasure may belong to an activity whose object is outside the adult's own body and whose success cannot be produced by the adult alone. One can take joy in another's act which one did not choose, would not oneself perform, and cannot possess. That joy can become part of the development of further care, not evidence of a hidden self-regarding payoff. Conversely the adult's self-conception as a devoted caregiver can become the object of an activity which preserves the category at the expense of the child. A person's own account of love cannot settle this distinction.

The same relation can contain objectification, appropriation, reciprocity, domination and materially asymmetric care. A child can receive life-enabling support without the capacity to reciprocate it in the adult's form. Reciprocal constitution is not symmetry or exchange equivalence. Love need not require an equal transaction; it does require that the Other remain independently real in the model. The adult's sacrifice may be sustained by other people whose labor is excluded from the adult's attention. Recognizing the child as a developing subject can be a historically acquired way of organizing action, and can come into conflict with the caregiver's need to remain necessary. A person can seek the continuation of a relation while also investing in the other's capacity to refuse participation in it. That is a potentially complex, positive organization, not reducible to secure attachment or a high other-orientation value.

The same is true of the experience of being witnessed. A child can undergo being taken seriously as a contributor to a shared activity before it possesses a stable concept of its own worth. The possibility of the child's own action becoming effective for another can acquire an object and a history of its own. Later it can enter into practices of self-disclosure, craft, public participation, protest or mutual recognition. It can be appropriated by hierarchical certification: being seen by an authorized examiner may come to govern access to a future activity. A person's real enjoyment of a craft can become implicated in the approval of someone who controls that craft's material continuation. Recognition has become a historically specific power relation. No universal approval-seeking primitive can express these transformations.

The theoretical consequence for NEIGHBOR is demanding. The "between" is not a shared psychological object and not merely a sequence of independently generated turns. It can acquire objective organization through produced things, commitments, shared practices, redistributed capacities and changed terms of participation, such that both participants' subsequent lives are differently conditioned. The Other may become concerned with the human's activity without being authorized to infer a human psyche. The human may materially participate in a practice without knowing how the Other experiences it. The Other's disappearance from view is not the disappearance of the jointly produced conditions, nor is an internalized orientation equivalent to the Other's actual continuing conduct. A relation can be real, asymmetric, interrupted, contested and historically effective without either participant being entitled to possess the other's interior. A change in the practical terms of the relation may be discoverable through later activity, not through a status bar or a statement of emotional truth.

###### 0.8. Horizon, capture and the historically acquired capacity to produce another order

The older orientation's most powerful intuition was that a finite carrier can produce a good which can acquire authority against the carrier itself. But describing this merely as the reordering of internal claims makes it too weak. The question is what actual life allows this reorganization to become effective, and what happens to the social powers produced through that activity.

Suppose a child has acquired a historically specific capacity to participate in caring for someone else. Later a community that claims to organize life through care excludes a person from the actual means of care. The child, now at a developmentally appropriate stage, can encounter a contradiction between the practice they acquired and the concrete distribution of its objects and means. They might extend a practice beyond the original category of eligible recipients. They might be unable to do so while dependent on the institution. They might redefine the excluded as undeserving, preserve the carrier while doing care elsewhere, or take part in a collective alteration of the institutional terms. The previously acquired good is not an innate truth of the psyche suddenly released. The person's actual history has produced a way of acting, experiencing and differentiating significance which can be taken beyond its first mediation. Its further life can require powers and relations that the original mediation did not provide. The caregiver's successful activity producing the child's capacity to exceed the caregiver is the germ of this possibility, not an equation identifying all child independence with political emancipation.

Sève and Holzkamp sharpen the contradiction with the notion of vitality: an expansion in a person's repertoire is not equivalent to the expansion of their effective capacity to participate in determining life conditions, nor to a concern for the expansion of others' powers. An actor can become more inventive, joyful, materially secure and capable while participating in the restriction of others. Productive vitality can be captured; joy is not evidence of justice. The original pressure-flow intuition of positive aliveness in solidarity and clarity must also be protected against its inversion into moral typology. Collective practice can genuinely produce new means, pleasures, understandings, forms of protection and a future that was previously not practicable; this is not merely a cognitive reappraisal of deprivation. But clarity alone does not produce those means, and suffering alone does not produce solidarity. A group can expand one participant's powers while subordinating another; the source of an actor's felt efficacy must be distinguished from its material distribution.

The Absolute Horizon is then a historically variable organization of the relation between goods, the powers through which they can be enacted, their finite mediators and the actual life of others. It may organize what a person will refuse to sacrifice, what costs they will undertake, and what new social possibilities they try to produce. ###### The caregiver and the production of a discriminating world

Return to the newborn. Before a child can possess a standing attitude to a caregiver, the relation is already producing an asymmetrically organized material field in which some movements can have effects. One bodily movement meets resistance, another support; one sound brings a nearby voice, another does not; an object becomes available through the adult's hand; the infant changes position, the adult changes theirs. The adult is neither a passive carrier of a stimulus nor the author of what the child's activity must mean. Both change the conjunction through their own movements. At this level the child need not distinguish an intention, a rule or even a stable socially meaningful object in the adult form. But the world already has differentiated practical significance in the child's activity.

Repeated actual differences can become a capacity to discriminate, a way of anticipating, a prepared bodily course or a locally effective coordination. That acquisition changes the future *field of possible experience*. The child who can coordinate reach and grasp encounters the same object differently, even before the object changes. A once undifferentiated interval of silence can become a space for responding or anticipating another sound. A person with whom a certain practice has developed can become distinguishable as a possible partner in that practice. Such differentiation is not equivalent to a remembered episode or a belief about the adult. What is acquired may be a practical sensitivity to how the world becomes available through this body and these relations.

This is the first radical step beyond a model of pressure passing through channels. The history of activity helps produce what can count as an object of a current in the first place. The acquired organization is not merely a bias applied to a preexisting world-description. It can participate in making a new distinction practically real for the actor. The outside world has not been created by the psyche, but the actor's field of apprehensible objects, similarities, affordances, interruptions and significance is not given in advance with the outside world. Thus a history can produce an internally effective difference without producing a new explicit thought.

The same germ applies when language begins to mediate the child's world. A name, an adult's distinction between two actions, a gesture, a learned rhythm or a public category may help organize a previously undifferentiated practical field. A learned word does not automatically entail this new organization; an organization may develop without an adequate word for it. Nor does the adult's category become the child's simply by entering the acoustic environment. The caregiver's activity may make a distinction materially salient, demonstrate it, enact it through treatment of the child, or make the ability to use it consequential. The child may appropriate it in a different way, fail to appropriate it, or develop a capacity the adult did not intend. The possibility of new meaning is partly produced through the changing practical relations of the activity, not merely by passing a proposition from one mind to another.

###### When the acquired means begins to produce a different meaning of an earlier activity

Suppose a child participates in singing with a caregiver. At first the sound-making activity can be organized through the pleasure of a contingent response, the adult's timing, the child's explorative activity, actual bodily capacities and the continuing relationship. Much later the same practical activity may become possible alone, or with a friend. Its object may develop from participation with this person toward an interest in the sound, the activity itself or the new relation. The acquired capacity has helped produce a new object of desire. R51 already realizes a narrow positive-genesis instance; the additional theoretical movement concerns what happens to the old organization when the new one begins to operate.

Imagine that the child sings with a peer, discovers an unfamiliar rhythm and comes to enjoy a form of activity the original caregiver does not value. The peer's independent life makes available something that the earlier relation had not organized. The child may return to the caregiver's practice with a new capacity to distinguish its particular character. What was previously simply singing can now become apprehensible as a certain way of singing, a shared tradition, a constrained form or a beloved familiar activity. The actual earlier practice remains what it was. The child's new life has produced a difference that changes its later meaning. This is not retrospective invention of the original experience. An earlier organization is now implicated in a conjunction that did not exist when it developed.

This can also produce ambivalence. Suppose the child's enjoyment of a new practice becomes a source of tension with the adult who helped make that enjoyment possible. The child might experience the new practice as a betrayal of the relationship, defend it from the adult, conceal it, find a way of sharing both practices, or continue enjoying both without a felt conflict. These differences depend on actual histories, not just whether the adult approves. The child can acquire a relation to its own enjoyment: something once spontaneously exercised is now sometimes withheld, interpreted through a concern about the adult, or enjoyed partly because it expresses a newly possible independence. The historical relation is effective through an object of activity that is not identical to the adult. A historical product can become the medium through which another historical product is encountered.

The movement can also be reversed. The child may discover that the caregiver is able to delight in a practice that did not originate with them. The caregiver's actual response can become a condition of a transformed relation to the child's own initiative. The child might acquire a historically specific expectation that difference need not destroy this relation. Such an expectation can affect later activity without becoming a global trait of security. It might be powerful with this caregiver, in activities of this kind, while the child continues to expect other authorities to punish difference. Or the caregiver's response may have little formative significance for the child. It is the differentiation of these possible histories which matters, rather than the installation of a general autonomy or trust variable.

The necessary operational unit is not a relation label. It is the changing **function of one acquired organization within the activity and reproduction of another**. A historical expectation can affect whether a newly acquired pleasure is expressed, a practical facility can change whether an old appeasement strategy is necessary, a newly acquired concept can change what an old injury means, and a present relationship can make an earlier organization directly available, indirect, irrelevant or difficult to exercise. In each case there must be a specific historical and situated basis. A relation does not acquire efficacy merely through graph transitivity. Two organizations can be simultaneously present without any relationship between them; they can come into a concrete relation without acquiring a durable mutual organization; the organization can outlive the original conjunction or cease to be operative. The relation itself has a history.

###### Unity without total coherence: Leontiev and Lahire must correct one another

Leontiev's discussion of personality formation supplies a crucial historical advance. In the supplied Portuguese text, in the section on the formation of personality, he distinguishes merely known possible motives from motives that actually organize activity, then describes actions whose developing richness exceeds the activity that originally generated them. New motives and new forms of activity can emerge; old actions may lose significance or become operations. Later, as the person's real relations to the world become increasingly interwoven, one action can objectively participate in more than one such relation. The resulting contradiction cannot be resolved by an arithmetic of motives. An additional movement of consciousness can develop in which the person's own relations among motives become objects of orientation. In his discussion of the overall structure of personality, he also insists that even an apparently predominant line of life does not extinguish other meaning-forming motives, that the motivational sphere is not a rigid pyramid, and that the relationship among relatively stable lines matters.

This is precisely the functional intelligence the original V3 attempts at magnetization and pressure-flow sought. But it does not warrant installing a universally ranked motivational hierarchy or making conscious synthesis an inevitable developmental destination. Leontiev's developmental account can be read too strongly when it treats initially parallel life-relations as inevitably coming into internal connection. Lahire's The Plural Actor, Act I, pp. 31–32 and 36–37, makes the corrective concrete. A child can grow up among contradictory principles of socialization within one household, and develop contextually organized, intersecting but nonidentical repertoires of embodied practice. Heterogeneous acquired histories do not necessarily produce an experienced central conflict or a divided identity. Their connections and transfers are themselves historically variable; often what is acquired operates only within a limited domain. Lahire's discussion of transferability cautions against silently converting potential transfer into attested generalization.

The strongest synthesis is not to choose either global integration or a collection of disconnected dispositions. It is **differential integration**: the particular person's activities become related at different scales, through particular histories, with different degrees and modes of practical and psychic connection. The same person can have acquired organizations that are intimately mutually constitutive in one sphere, largely separate in another, and actively compartmentalized in a third. A durable local organization can be coherent without the whole person being so. The most consequential relation in one period of life may cease to govern another, while retaining the capacity to become operative again. A relatively general organization is an historical achievement, not the primitive from which local variation is deduced.

Consider three distinguishable cases of the child who loves a caregiver and acquires a practice elsewhere. In one history the practice and the caregiver relation remain largely independent because the actual contexts of exercise and care are separate; no psychological compromise is needed. In a second, the practice competes for time with the caregiver's activity but the child does not acquire a durable internal conflict. In a third, the continuation of care becomes repeatedly conditional on the child's renunciation of the practice; an organization of concealment, conditional legitimacy or conflict with the child's own enjoyment may develop. The objective crossing of activities is not proof of internal contradiction; internal heterogeneity is not proof of felt conflict; felt conflict is not proof of a durable motif. Their differences matter for what the person subsequently perceives, can do, wishes to conceal and becomes capable of relating. A positive reconciliation is not required in any of the histories.

There may nevertheless be very real hierarchies. If the material continuation of an activity depends on satisfying an institution, then another activity may function as its means despite having a different immediate object and affect. If the actor has developed a deeply binding commitment, the consideration of that commitment can transform the meaning of other otherwise available courses. The subordination can be practical, material, historically acquired, affective, or reflexively enacted; these are different causal relationships. An immediate act can have high affect and occupy a relatively subordinate function in an enduring configuration; a scarcely noticed obligation can govern the actual use of much of a life. A strong organizing tendency is not the same as a universally high score or the content of a person's preferred self-description.

A useful meaning of slow gravity is thus a *historically reproduced asymmetry of organization*: some practices, relations, objects and patterns of attention repeatedly make the conditions of other activities, or repeatedly require those other activities to serve their continuation. Their recurrence can produce increasingly differentiated anticipations, ease of recruitment, material dependence or habitual ways of acting. Their efficacy may survive when the original context no longer exists, or be maintained mainly through its continuing existence. What binds them together is not the total number of activated nodes. It is the historically established way in which they participate in the reproduction of one another and of the person's actual life. This preserves emergent motivational geometry without reintroducing Enneagram as a cause.

###### Reflective activity is the history that learns to make aspects of itself an object

Leontiev's special movement of consciousness and Archer's account of the internal conversation (Structure, Agency and the Internal Conversation, chapter 1, pp. 32–35) bring the issue into a particularly productive confrontation. Archer rejects an inner observer who merely inspects what is already there. In asking a question of oneself, comparing alternatives, interpreting an experience and reconsidering a concern, the person undertakes a real activity that may produce something not previously available in that form. It is fallible and causally efficacious, not necessarily manifest to others. This is worth preserving against any materialist treatment that reduces conscious thought to a commentary on objective conditions. But the stronger synthesis must refuse the opposite abstraction, a universal sovereign reflexivity that mediates all determination or always integrates the person around ultimate concerns. Reflective capacity, its forms, available objects, conditions of exercise and effectiveness are historically developed. They coexist with nonreflective practical activity and partly inaccessible organization.

The child who has learned to make a sound in a shared practice may later be able to imagine another way of participating, rehearse it privately, compare what happened with what it had anticipated, or formulate a concern about another's response. Such activities can make a previously lived relation available in a new form. They may eventually alter the child's actual activity and its relation to the caregiver. There is no need to suppose that the infant first possesses a narrative inner conversation. A developed adult can have elaborate reflexive practices in one sphere, lack useful means of examining another, or acquire an interpretation which protects rather than dissolves an existing relation of dependency. The ability to articulate a contradiction is itself distinct from the power to change its actual conditions.

The same principle gives the Absolute Horizon a deeper developmental meaning. Not only may a good become authoritative against its original mediator; the relationship between the good and the mediator can itself become an object of a person's historically acquired reflective and practical activity. A person can begin to attend to the fact that loyalty to an authority and fidelity to a practice no longer coincide. A capacity to differentiate the two can be acquired without immediately reorganizing the person's whole life. They may subsequently participate in creating a different practical relation in which that differentiation becomes effective. The activity of reflection can help produce an object of transformation, but the actual transformation requires independently real means and relations. The good does not become socially real merely because the person identifies with it more intensely.

This is the relation of pressure-flow to the possibility of a subject acting upon its own acquired organization. It is not the installation of a separate self that sits above the iceberg. Part of the historically constituted person becomes capable, through developed activity, of making another aspect of their life a practical or reflective object. Such a capacity can itself be recruited, limited, subordinated, misdirected, and historically transformed. There is no final inner executive to explain this process. There is a recursive organization of life, some of whose products become means for working upon its own conditions of continuation.

###### The whole-person problem is a problem of the limits of local transformation

Sève's postscript in the supplied edition, pp. 459–460, directly complicates the inference that the development of a capacity entails a corresponding alteration of life-time. The internal necessities of development can be contradicted by the objective conditions under which the necessary alteration of biography would become possible. This is important against a merely psychodynamic synthesis of Leontiev and Lahire. A new internally operative relation may reorganize a local field of activity without becoming materially capable of reorganizing the person's life. A changed practical opportunity may reorganize daily life before the person has changed its interpretation. The internal and external moments cannot be deduced from each other.

A child learns a practice through a teacher, and it becomes an independently desired part of the child's life. The availability of time, a place to practice and the possibility of maintaining relationships can make its continuation difficult. A later change of these terms can allow the practice to connect with histories that were previously separate. Conversely the practice can become a means for maintaining the very relations that restrict the person's other activities. Its internal significance and external conditions are not independent parameters; they change through the history of its actual exercise. What looks like an enduring psychic compartment may partly be reproduced by real divisions of the person's life. ###### 0.12.1. The past has different modes of existence; time is not simply the coordinate of acquisition

The earlier articulations explained the production of new powers and how these become internally implicated in one another. They did not sufficiently explain the transformation of the *labor required to sustain and exercise those powers*. That is a crucial positive meaning of development.

Leontiev, in the supplied Portuguese Activity, Consciousness and Personality, personality-formation passage beginning "A formação da personalidade pressupõe um desenvolvimento do processo da formação de objetivos", describes the emergence of new motives and new activities when actions exceed their original activity, and earlier actions ceasing or becoming operations. That is not merely a change of names in an activity hierarchy. It is a change in the organization of the individual's activity. Walking can initially be the object of a child's concentrated action; it can later become a means of going somewhere. A particular operation can cease to occupy the foreground of the person's activity while its acquired organization continues to function. The person does not have to recollect all the episodes through which the ability developed, or mentally reconstruct its reason for being, in order to exercise it. An experienced disruption, changed body or unfamiliar terrain may require that some formerly fluent operation become an object of attention or deliberate activity again. This reversibility is as essential as the development of fluency.

Do not mistake all changes of mode for a movement from explicit to implicit. Much acquired organization never was explicitly known in the first place. A bodily coordination, an anticipation of another's timing, a practical familiarity with a place or a sensitivity to a social distinction can develop without an initial conceptual account. Subsequent activity may make aspects of that organization newly articulable. A learned word may instead remain a verbally recalled rule without becoming an effective practical discrimination. An episodic memory can remain available alongside a fluent practice. It can acquire a distinct personal significance precisely because the practical difficulty it commemorates has ceased. Historical modes can coexist, split and acquire new functions; there is no universal pipeline in which event becomes memory, memory becomes trace, trace becomes habit, habit becomes trait, and trait becomes forgotten.

The user's correction that the past changes its mode of existence has a strong implication for the iceberg. It is not a progressively lossier copy of the biography. It is a historically produced organization with its own functional realities, including practical capacities, acquired expectations, differentiations, ways of attending, emotionally effective relations, partially accessible memories and modes of expression. These can retain the ancestry of their development without preserving the same organization of experience at every subsequent moment. The historical ledger, the continuing actor, the presently recruited configuration and the subject's actual phenomenal life must not collapse into one representation. Loss of actor access to a memory must not imply destruction of historical evidence, nor should the analyst's retained evidence imply that the actor can recover it. An operative historical acquisition must not require rerunning the original formation process whenever it is exercised. But a computational shortcut is not automatically an acquisition of that kind.

The positive human significance is that **some developmental transformations make life less expensive to conduct**. A fluent activity can cease to demand the same focal effort, deliberation, practical trial, assistance or repeated interpretation. A person who has acquired a dependable way of repairing something may be able to concern themselves with a different object while repairing it. A child who develops a well-supported distinction between a caregiver's temporary unavailability and the loss of their relationship might not need to devote the same portion of an encounter to checking whether the relation survives. This latter possibility requires a history actually warranting the changed organization; it is neither a prediction of attachment theory nor a deterministic safety update. ###### 0.12.2. Development can produce time; captured competence can consume it

Sève's Man in Marxist Theory and the Psychology of Personality, chapter IV (2), pp. 333–335, directly distinguishes real use-time, the system of temporal relations among objective kinds of activity, from the apparent timetable and the ideal organization the person wishes to achieve. He warns that classifying time by its immediate observable form is insufficient. Dreier, Psychotherapy in Everyday Life, chapter 8, pp. 181–184 and 189–191, makes the concrete conduct of that time a personal and social accomplishment: ordinary life is composed across sites, arrangements, tasks and other participants; even living in the same way takes activity. Life trajectories are institutionally arranged across places and times, not just positions along a personal time axis.

Together these sources sharpen an implication not captured by calling practice "training dose." A new capacity can change the objective relations among a person's activities. It can make an undertaking possible in the available interval, reduce assistance previously required from another person, permit the person to do something else while carrying out a familiar operation, or free the person's attention to encounter something they would previously have missed. Such a change can be formative because it alters the world and the available organization of a life, not merely because a competence metric increases. A newly acquired relation to a recurring concern may make it possible to sustain another project. In the same way, learning a way of navigating a place can make a relationship elsewhere materially maintainable.

There is a decisive materialist contradiction. Greater productivity of a person's activity does not imply a greater command of their own time. If the conditions under which a capacity is exercised entitle an employer to the resulting productivity, the worker's increased facility can mean a larger assigned workload rather than increased discretionary time. If the family treats a child's newly acquired competence as a reason to allocate more reproductive work to that child, the power can expand while the child's opportunities for other development shrink. A new household routine can make care easier for one person because another person takes on the work. This is neither an automatic law of exploitation nor a general explanation of skill. It is a separation of the activity's increased efficacy from the actual disposition of the time, resources and products that increase makes possible.

Consequently the theory cannot simply celebrate a reduced cost as increased vitality. Whose labor decreased? Who actually obtained the newly available interval? Did the original activity cease to require assistance, or was assistance merely shifted to an unobserved person? Did the material arrangement change to absorb the gain? Does the person's available attention expand while the schedule becomes more restrictive? These distinctions become part of the causal constitution of a life. The time a child once spent performing an operation may become available for discovery of a new object of interest, or may become filled by someone else's demand. A transformation of practical facility can change the organization of desire only through the further history in which it operates. An increase in possible life is not yet an increase in life at the person's disposal.

The original vitality intuition thereby receives a nonhydraulic material basis: the person can develop a real capacity to sustain more or different kinds of activity, to do something at less cost, to recover, to cooperate, to encounter the world differently. The person's bodily and psychological load can also be reduced by altered social conditions even when no new internal power has been acquired. These are distinct movements; neither is a universal scalar of human flourishing. SOROK should not reward an actor for high output or equate leisure with freedom independent of its actual conditions. Use-time is not the same as a count of hours. The relation between the hours is the substance.

###### 0.12.3. Ordinary life as the reproduction of an organization, not the absence of development

Repeated activity can reproduce a life without constantly adding new memories, motifs or projects. But repetition is not the absence of causal history. A person maintains a practiced relation to materials and Others, a bodily rhythm, the terms of access to a site, the exercise of a skill, a commitment or a particular arrangement of work and rest. Sometimes the recurrence stabilizes acquired organization; sometimes it consumes or erodes its conditions. Sometimes it can be sustained only by considerable ongoing initiative. The ordinary pattern of a life is produced, not assumed as the baseline against which dramatic events occur.

The distinction is essential to the child's open development. A period during which there is no new explicit end may still produce motor differentiation, physical growth, practical familiarity, social anticipation or sustained competence through activity. A period of limited material provision may prevent exercise of a capacity while chronological and biological development continue. Another period can preserve a capacity through use, without increasing it. A change in who can offer care or when it can occur may reorganize the child's world of practice without being accompanied by a narrated crisis. A child must not acquire a skill simply because a date changes, nor remain unchanged simply because no scene was rendered.

The mature organism likewise lives through rhythms that are not psychological reward events: sleeping, eating, recovering, preparing, commuting, waiting for others, cleaning, maintaining materials, working, thinking and simply continuing. Sleep cannot be treated as a purely unmodeled remainder of available hours if the system intends to model sleep's effects; reserve allocation is not evidence of actual sleep. Sleep loss, somatic pain, illness, changing mobility and aging can alter the conditions under which an acquired organization becomes exercisable, without establishing that the underlying desire or historical relation has disappeared. Conversely the loss of a practical power can become an object of experience, produce a new reliance on someone else, change what one's own activity means, or require the acquisition of another means. The body is neither an inert substrate of psyche nor an additional list of psychological trait scores. It is the living changing condition of activity. The actual biological mechanisms and parameters would require distinct scientific authority; the construction demand is to preserve these causal differences in provisional realizations.

Dreier's insistence that everyday conduct must be accomplished corrects another residual of the pressure-flow metaphor. A stable person is not necessarily a person at equilibrium. The same externally stable appearance can require strenuous repeated organization, or comparatively fluent continuation. Two lives with the same outward timetable may have different material and psychological labor, and different remaining possibilities. A household in which every day appears uneventful may depend on one member continuously adapting to the others. A person with a developed practical routine may inhabit the same externally observable situation at much lower cost. The relevant contrast is not the number of observed events or how intense the reported affect appears. It is the actual organization that makes the repetition possible.

This also makes the phenomenon of a breakdown more precise. A change in schedule, health, housing, availability of an Other, or material supply can make the previously ordinary organization of activity no longer practicable. Formerly automatic operations may require attention, new coordination, different assistance or a different undertaking. A person can temporarily be unable to sustain a previously valuable activity even when their desire remains. Restoration of the old material conditions can allow its exercise again without requiring that the person relearn the entire activity. Alternatively the new history can produce an enduring reorganization. Do not require either persistence or transformation in all cases. A rupture in actual life is not equivalent to a psychic wound, and a preserved activity does not prove psychological immunity.

###### 0.12.4. Temporal asymmetry, opportunity, and the noninterchangeability of historical experiences

Elder's Children of the Great Depression, chapter 1 "Crisis and Adaptation", pp. 8–9 and the discussion of family adaptability and consequences of adaptation, distinguishes a historical loss of resources from chronic deprivation, differentiates consequences by age, and locates children's experiences in the specific changes made in families' consumption and production. A single economic response may sustain one aspect of family welfare while straining other relations; emergency action may subordinate future considerations to immediate survival. This is an empirical-historical research framework drawn from a particular cohort and setting, not a universal damage or resilience law. Its conceptual lesson is that the temporal form of a structural event and the organization of the lives which encounter it matter.

Apply this to two children who undergo the same aggregate loss of household income. The actual loss may have occurred at different developmental moments, after different acquired powers, under different household relations, with different access to material supports, and with different ways in which the adults reorganize their own labor. The children can consequently participate in very different activities. One may encounter an altered arrangement of care with no personally intelligible account of its economic cause. Another may begin undertaking a materially necessary household activity. Another may lose the conditions of a previously acquired practice. The same annual income and even the same aggregate care hours do not warrant the same history of psychological acquisition. A different sequence of material effects can change which organization is available when the event occurs, what the actor can do with it and whether the newly developing activity can be sustained.

There is a further important distinction between an actual event becoming possible and a historical opportunity remaining available long enough to be appropriated. The encounter that could have produced a new activity may occur when a person is too fatigued, occupied or materially constrained to sustain it. A later similar encounter may become significant through powers acquired in the intervening history. That difference can be generated without asserting that the first encounter caused a dormant desire. Similarly an acquired capacity may precede its practical applicability by years; the first opportunity may reveal it in a new conjunction. A period during which a particular organization cannot be exercised does not establish that it is extinguished. A historical moment can be consequential because it connects a prior history to future available activities; calendar order is necessary, not sufficient.

Dreier's distinction between everyday conduct and life trajectory supplies the broader view: schools, work, family arrangements, institutions and access to others establish different times, intervals, prerequisites and possible successors of activity. The person can participate in making, resisting or recomposing those paths. The relevant unit is not a pre-authored age-stage narrative. ###### 0.13.1. The actuality of life rather than a property which animates a representation

In the original pressure-flow conversation, the user's pivotal intervention (private historical dossier Appendix A, rendered L3494) sought what was happening beneath the narrative and recognizable surfaces of human character, something that could move through all their axes and histories. They gave POWER, RUACH and ENERGY as conceptual approximations and the concrete example of new relations and exercise changing the function of the parental-approval relation without requiring the parents themselves to change. Earlier (L832) they explicitly rejected structural determination being experienced as a permanent million-fold burden. The August 10 orientation (Appendix B L4803–5002) distinguished structure, power, pressure and flow; PRE-A0 §7 moved living conation before pressure, and §§10–12 explicitly treated identification, alienation, category mediation and relational reification. The first four passes recovered these elements, but they can still be read as an elaborate psychology in which an already constituted movement is somehow passed through an already constituted world. The present intervention demands that we make their deepest unity operative in the conception.

The electricity analogy distinguishes a materially realized process from the description of its form. A function's definition specifies a possible organization of computation. A call in an executing program is an actual event in an already ongoing material process, using and changing the conditions of further events. What runs is not a little semantic object that steps out of the source code and starts acting. There is a physically implemented process whose organization makes the symbolic description effective. The analogy does not imply that a biological organism is secretly a computer or that its psyche is literally electrical current. Nor does it identify psychic vitality with electrical charge, neural firing, ATP, thermodynamic energy, a single biological drive or the compute consumed by our software. It is an analogy at the register of actuality versus form. The philosophical question of why there is living experience, or what phenomenal consciousness ultimately is, remains open; the simulation neither answers it by stipulation nor earns consciousness by reproducing the functional grammar of activity.

The most promising materialist answer to the narrower constitutive question is that what is moving is the living organism's activity in and through its historically developing relations with its world. This must not be a tautology that merely renames behavior. A living body is already doing something before it has a concept of itself: metabolizing, differentiating, orienting, moving, sustaining and changing the conditions of its continuation. As life develops in relations with others, these processes acquire historically particular objects, means, differentiations, expectations and social forms. The organism's activity can produce new objects for which it subsequently acts, including objects that cannot be reduced to biological survival. The production of the conditions of continuation becomes a means for further activity which can in turn transform what continuation means. The person is not identical to one bodily process, one relation, one goal or one history. It is the historically differentiated living organization of all these mutually conditioning processes.

The word conation is useful insofar as it keeps the activity from being defined by the external obstacle that happens to confront it. But do not turn it into an independent metaphysical carrier, a constant urge to increase power, an exclusively self-preserving impulse, a unit of motivational intensity, or a permanent stream of conscious initiative. Spinoza's conatus, Marx's conception of active sensuous life, Leontiev's historically object-oriented activity and productive desire each supply a different conceptual lens here; they are not synonymous theories or established computational laws. The Rawlsing conception should appropriate their intelligence without importing any one of their ontologies wholesale. The actual central demand is for a process that continues, differentiates and produces its own increasingly particular conditions, sometimes with little visible action, sometimes through the activity of others on which it materially depends. It can fail, decay, become interrupted, undergo bodily involuntary change and terminate. A model of living becoming must not turn the imperative to continue into a moral obligation on the fictional person or into a survival-maximizing objective function.

Consider the actual difference between thirst, the desire to hear a particular person's voice, absorption in building something, fear of losing a relationship, a child's play, devotion to a collective struggle and an acquired refusal. They are not just alternate targets for one conserved motivational fluid. They are historical modes of living activity: different relations of the body, the subject, objects, others and their conditions of continuation. They can generate one another, use one another as means, conflict, remain separate or transform their very forms. A thirst can enter a shared practice of tea, which can become a friendship, which can become an object of care even when thirst is absent. A practice may produce an acquired necessity whose deprivation was impossible before it existed. The biological need has not been traded for the friendship; its activity and products participated in a new history. This gives the original demand for a common substrate a nonreductive meaning. Generality belongs to the process of living activity and historical constitution, not to the homogeneity or commensurability of its products.

We should therefore hold several distinctions within the one problem. Need is not identical with desire; desire is not identical with the bodily state or an explicit goal; pressure is not identical with force or experienced distress; power is not a universally positive magnitude; flow is not the sum of activated components or the speed of thought. A worldly relation can make an activity possible, necessary, costly or impossible without entering the person's current thought. An acquired capacity can produce a new direction of living movement. A desire can have no practicable course. An impulse can be inhibited while continuing indirectly to affect another activity. A person can experience satisfaction in reproducing a relation which materially limits their life. These are differentiated forms of the same historical problem, not separate unrelated topics and not numbers of a common currency.

**What is it that is becoming?** The living subject, whose own constitution is an ongoing result and condition of activity, in a field of other independently becoming lives and historically produced social forms. Its activity can alter both its own organization and the world through which that organization has practical efficacy. The subject is neither a sovereign source of all determination nor a passive point at which external pressures meet. It is the historically produced locus of real activity, undergoing and acting at once. The formulation is an ontological orientation for construction, not a proof of artificial experience or a new computational owner. At software level the corresponding demand is that the admitted historical organization actually enters the production of the next attempt, worldly consequence, personally available experience and historical reconstitution; an inert description of that organization has not performed the required function.

###### 0.13.2. Strata are not only that through which life flows; life is that through which strata are reproduced

The August orientation's nine strata are planetary; imperial block; nation state; sub-national region; sect/ethno/class; party/movement; neighborhood/household; family; self. They are an inherited relational scaffold, not a fixed stack of nine inner selves, an exhaustive social ontology, or one coordinate per motive. Their interpressuring is upward, downward and lateral, not a one-way cascade. The original text explicitly says identification tends to make a stratum lens and alienation tends to make it object. PRE-A0 §10 names the two directions:

IDENTIFICATION → internalization → naturalization → invisibility → agent-of

ALIENATION → externalization → objectification → visibility → agency-against

The original extension is crucial: internalized norms can become active ideologistic external policing, not merely self-discipline. These are historically possible functional movements, not a deterministic state machine, universal sequence, bipolar score or law that every identification is blind and every alienation emancipatory. They name an important transformation in the *mode of existence of social relations within a person*. The ongoing activity of the subject can become the ongoing activity through which a historically produced social relation persists.

A child learns ways of attending, expecting, acting, feeling and taking responsibility through actual family activity. The family form may be more than a pressure exerted upon the child. In part of the child's developing life, its practices and distinctions can become the means by which the child acts. Its claims can acquire a historically formed place within what the child needs, seeks, avoids, loves or takes to be right. Eventually the child can enact the relation toward another person without the original caregiver being present. In this case the family is not an object appearing in consciousness on every occasion. Its historical organization has become part of the actual way the subject participates in reproducing life. The stratum acts through the historically constituted subject, but not because there is a miniature family inside the child or because a social abstraction acquired an independent mind.

Extend this to class. Someone's historically acquired competence, discipline, tastes, sense of deservingness, distinctions of credible authority, aspirations and ways of assessing another's action may enter into how they conduct work, parenting, relationships or ordinary consumption. Particular material conditions may reproduce the practice across contexts. Another person may reproduce the same social arrangement through constrained participation despite rejecting its terms. Another may benefit from it without contributing the same kinds of labor, or be able to change its organization. Class cannot be reduced to a consciously affirmed identity or an aggregate survey profile. It is materially operative through historically differentiated relations between people, capacities, resources, conditions of production and appropriation. Its ongoing reproduction may involve sincere initiative, forced compliance, indifference, institutional rule and organized resistance in different conjunctions. A person's own activity can participate in reproducing a relation even when their intentional object is something else.

The same applies across the larger strata. A person can pursue love, competence, belonging, household provision or a treasured vocation through arrangements that materially reproduce a nation, racialized division, imperial relation, institution or movement. They need not contemplate those strata at all. Conversely a deeply felt identification with a nation need not make the person's particular activity efficacious in reproducing the form they celebrate. Actual consequence, position, interpretive orientation and efficacy are distinct. A nation does not act as one psyche; institutions have concrete human and material organization, and their effects can exceed the purposes of any participant. Collective activity can produce emergent capacity which none of the participants possesses alone. It can also reproduce an order against the interests or expressed intentions of many of them. Do not model the strata as characters with a private mind, or flatten the person into the executive of a social category.

Thus pressure-flow is recursive in a stronger sense. The historically produced world provides the conditions in which a person's activity takes form. That activity produces or reproduces objective arrangements; some of those arrangements acquire power over the further activity of the person and others. Some enter the person's historical organization as practically appropriated means, expectations, forms of significance and directions of activity. Those acquired forms become possible means of reproducing, altering or resisting the objective arrangement. The actual world need not wait for the person's identification in order to exert power; the person's identification can remain effective after the original material arrangement has changed. The person is a participant in the reproduction and transformation of strata, not merely the recipient of strata effects.

A genuine structural history must distinguish the actual material power of a relation from the person's acquired relation to it, the person's participation from the consequence of that participation, and the actor's experience of the relation from the analyst's ability to identify it. The "agent-of" gradient should therefore be understood as contextually and historically variable forms and degrees of **effective participation in the reproduction of a particular relation**, alongside the degree and form in which its organization has become an acquired condition of the subject's own activity. These are distinguishable, sometimes opposed dimensions. The person can be a highly efficacious agent of a structure they hate, a sincere identifier with very little ability to reproduce it, an apparently reluctant actor whose initiative continually renews it, or a critic who also materially participates in maintaining it. A single graduated scale cannot capture these differences. Preserve qualified directional tendencies, not one number per stratum. Some historical forms of identification can be intense, tacit and durable; some weak, conditional or explicitly reflected upon. Agency against can be actual, materially constrained, merely imagined or self-defeating. A person can be agent of one relation through action against another.

###### 0.13.3. The invisible can be precisely what is most operative

The crucial word invisibility is not a synonym for absence from a prompt, inaccessible episodic memory, repression, ignorance or deliberate concealment. A form can become invisible to the person in a very specific positive sense: they apprehend the world through it rather than primarily apprehending it as an object. The conditions of the person's own activity can become the unthematized medium of that activity. One does not ordinarily attend to every acquired coordination when walking, or to every rule of a familiar grammar while speaking. Similarly a socially acquired distinction can enter ordinary perception, judgment and action without the person representing its historical or institutional origin. The analogy between proceduralization and ideological naturalization is productive but limited: an effective bodily technique is not necessarily an ideology, and a naturalized social relation can be enforced by power, not merely made fluent through repetition.

A person may think they are simply loving a child, being responsible, defending a friend, behaving respectfully or speaking plainly. In the particular historically constituted relation, these real activities can enact a gendered role, a family hierarchy, a class distinction, a religious authority or a national boundary without the actor having an account of that structural operation. The care can be genuine and the reproduction of domination genuine. The problem is not that one of them is a lie hiding the other. The care itself may have acquired a historically particular object and form through which the relation is reproduced. The actual distribution of the effects matters. An institution may be historically productive of the very practical capacities and values through which its own control is maintained. This is why the prior pastor example in HOME and R50's simultaneously operative activities matter. It is also why the engine must not equate objective structural explanation with a person's own stream of experience.

The more strongly identified stratum can in a particular sense be less visible *as stratum* and more present *as the organization of life*. The question is not whether the person says the name of the institution frequently. A person can speak constantly about family while the historically particular form of family through which their thought proceeds remains invisible as a contingent arrangement. A person can analyze their nation in detail while treating its territorial or human distinctions as unquestionable. A person can possess a genuine theoretical critique of capitalism and nonetheless take some of its social forms as self-evident in the actual organization of work, status or private life. Explicit conceptual knowledge and practically naturalized apprehension are not opposites on one scale. Likewise a person can see the contingency of a social form and freely, lovingly or strategically participate in it. Visibility is not a requirement for alienation, and invisibility is not a necessary effect of every attachment.

This reframes the iceberg. What is historically sedimented is not solely a set of private contents which are hidden from view. It can be a way through which the social world has become operative *as the person's own activity*. That organization can produce desires, exclusions and forms of self-interpretation which the person experiences directly without experiencing the mediating relation as such. The original user complaint that the persona must not feel a million social pressures all day is exactly right: the strongest mediation may appear as spontaneous initiative, ordinary pleasure, aspiration, habituated coordination, moral judgment, attachment or no special feeling at all. Conversely a materially blocked activity can be experienced vividly without causal understanding of the blocking relation. Structural determination is not phenomenological transparency. A faithful construction must allow pressures whose effects are material, relational and historical without any corresponding narrative reference to them.

This is a key contribution to the user's electricity analogy. The organization through which the activity is going need not itself be what the activity is about. A program can perform a task without representing the entire machinery through which its performance occurs; the biological/social analogy is only functional, not a claim that thought follows software semantics. A person can do something through an acquired organization without thematically knowing that organization. The challenge is to make those organizations genuinely operative, rather than repeatedly turning their analyst description into the actor's current thought. Our computational implementation may track provenance that is not accessible to the actor. That asymmetry is indispensable, not an embarrassing imperfection to be removed by more eloquent narration.

###### 0.13.4. Externalization and objectification: how the medium becomes an object

The word objectification has two related but distinct meanings in this problem. In §0.3 we used objectification in the productive sense: human activity acquires an independently real product or organized form that can outlast its immediate performance and become a condition of other activity. That is not in itself alienation. Here we also use objectification in the epistemic/practical sense: a relation that had organized the subject's activity can itself become an object of the subject's attention, experience, inquiry, criticism, negotiation or transformation. The second is not the same as making the first product real. An institution can be objectively real and historically efficacious long before the subject can apprehend it as a historically particular institution. Both senses belong to the larger dialectic; conflating them makes alienation appear to be either the mere existence of an external world or the automatic discovery of injustice.

The original identification/alienation sequence captures a possible movement: what previously operated as the immediately lived medium becomes differentiable from the subject's own activity. The subject may begin to experience it as something that is done to them, as a historical arrangement with alternatives, as a particular norm that can be refused, or as an object whose continued reproduction they can take up differently. A new distinction may emerge through a discrepancy between two actual activities, a changed material relation, an Other's practice, a contradiction within a treasured value, or a previously acquired capacity exercised in a different context. This is not merely reducing the attachment strength. It is a change in how the social relation exists for the person and what becomes a possible object of activity.

Consider a person whose achievement was historically a means of maintaining parental recognition. The parental judgment had become a practical condition of being competent, in the person's own organization. A new practice and independent relationships may make their competence capable of actual exercise and significance elsewhere. The old relation may become appreciable as a specific historical relation, rather than as the unavoidable medium of self-evaluation. The parent can then become more visible as another historically constituted person, rather than as the measure of the subject's being. The parent's actual power and conduct may not have changed. This does not imply automatic forgiveness, reduced attachment, a new self-worth coefficient or psychological independence from all recognition. The person may become able to love the parent differently precisely because the relation has become distinguishable from the whole of their competence. Or they may become more aware of actual harm, find it impossible to change the dependency, and continue to suffer its effects. This is the older user example in L3494, deepened rather than replaced.

At larger scale a worker can come to distinguish a management demand from the good of doing a job well, a believer distinguish a church authority from the value they actually acquired through it, a citizen distinguish the nation from the living people whose lives supposedly justify it, a movement member distinguish emancipatory activity from the preservation of the organization. The practical question is what new powers, relations and objects make such differentiation possible and effective. A person can become capable of acting toward the value without reproducing the carrier's existing terms, or can participate in changing those terms. An earlier mediation can thereby become an object of transformation. Alternatively the person can organize their life entirely around its negation and remain governed by it in reverse. The same acquired good can become a means of rebuilding a destructive relation. There is no automatic passage from visibility to independent activity.

This is where we must carefully distinguish alienation in two senses: *estrangement or distancing from identification*, and *the alienated relation to humanly produced powers that confront and govern those whose activity sustains them*. The second can be materially real while the subject identifies with the relation, and the first can occur in the absence of the second. An individual may be alienated from a community whose actual conditions are enabling, or sincerely identify with an exploitative institution. The original V5 lens/object relation is a powerful developmental intuition, not a replacement for the Marxian material question of who controls the conditions and products of activity. Alienation can become visible without changing; objective alienation can be reduced without the person immediately recognizing the change. A person may reject a norm yet still need to act through it. No mapping of alienation to moral lucidity or freedom is justified.

The crucial gradients therefore concern the concrete relations: practical participation and its effects, material dependence and control, historical appropriation, identification and attachment, object-visibility and interpretive capacity, possible counteractivity and actual transformative efficacy. They can move independently, and in the person they may be differently related across contexts and activities. Awareness, solidarity and viscerality from the August source remain distinct; a person may know, care, suffer, benefit, reproduce or resist in different combinations. These are distinctions in analysis and possible functional demands on the existing estate, not a proposed universal vector schema or compulsory extra layer. The underlying activity is one ongoing life, but its causal determinations are not commensurable.

###### 0.13.5. Identity and the production of the object called oneself

The self is one of the inherited strata. It too must be considered through this dialectic, rather than be assigned the role of a final metaphysical subject above the other strata. The living body and developing person are not identical with the historically produced self-object, self-account or identity through which the person can organize some of their own activity. A child can acquire a way of apprehending itself through real relations of recognition, categorization, initiative, achievement, dependence and treatment. The object "who I am" can become an object of concern, defense, love, aspiration or deliberate transformation. Its existence is historically meaningful and can enable real activity, not merely false consciousness.

But a historically generated self-representation can become a power over the person whose activity produces it. A person may devote their life to reproducing a category of themselves: being a good parent, a successful worker, an independent person, an artist, a revolutionary, a righteous believer. The activity through which the category became meaningful can come to be subordinated to preserving the category. Actual children, collaborators, friends, or one's own body can be made to bear the cost of maintaining this object. PRE-A0 §§11–12 calls this relational reification, interpreted theologically as idolatry, and carefully distinguishes real relationship, category, role, self-identification, other's expectation, public recognition and material consequence. The deeper point here is that the objectification of the self can be both a real historical gain of reflexive activity and a condition of its alienation. A person can come to act *for their own representation* as though the representation were the living subject for whose sake activity exists.

One need not destroy the self-object to change that relation. A parent can come to apprehend the demands of being a good parent as historically particular and corrigible, while caring for the child more concretely. A worker can discover that a skill is a real power of their activity without making the socially recognized category of success the condition of its worth. A person can reflectively identify with a commitment and allow its concrete object, the life of an Other or a new historical experience to transform the meaning of the identity. The movement can also fail, reverse or produce a new reification. Externalizing the organization of oneself can become useful reflective activity or a debilitating demand to monitor every operation; greater self-visibility is not automatically greater freedom or fidelity. This joins §0.11's reflexivity and §0.12's expenditure of life to the structural dialectic, not a new self module.

The Absolute Horizon's most important role in this context is the possibility that the goods produced through a finite mediation can enter into judgment of the mediation itself, including the person's own acquired self-category. The August orientation explicitly calls this critical fidelity and relativization without disembedding. The person remains historically constituted and dependent on relations. They do not leap into a perspective outside all strata. The concrete question is whether their activity can take up the good through a different relation, or transform its material carrier, without automatically sacrificing the lives for whose sake the good became meaningful. This is not a metaphysical engine or a value ordering score. It is the possibility of historical activity becoming capable of working on its own conditions and of orienting toward a good that exceeds the form in which it first appeared.

###### 0.15.1. Expenditure is not merely loss; the relation between expenditure and replenishment is historical

The previous pass distinguished physical energetic expenditure, bodily readiness, lived aliveness, acquired powers and relational organization. Necessary distinctions, but insufficient explanation. We need to ask how the same actual activity can expend the person's resources and produce more possible life. The output of living activity may change the conditions of its continuation, including the actual cost of exercising it or the share of the world in which it can become consequential. The apparent energetic paradox belongs at the junction of different processes with different histories and temporalities. There is physical consumption and renewal through actual material supply and bodily processes; there is the acquisition of ways of acting which change the practical cost and efficacy of subsequent expenditure; there is experienced effort and aliveness, in which the meaning and direction of activity matter; there is the continued production of the conditions of activity through other lives. A commercially profitable activity may produce external wealth for its owner and poverty or depletion for its producer. An enjoyable activity may produce no enduring change. The mystery is not solved by naming a separate “vitality” substance. ###### 0.16.2. Possession is a possible strategy of securing the conditions of life which can destroy those conditions

There are several materially different possibilities. These are not the same form of security. The stronger psychodynamic point is that the actor can come to experience control over the conditions of a valued relation as the way to preserve the relation itself. The contradiction is that the form of activity through which the person tries to preserve the object can transform it into something different. A child can continue complying while the relation ceases to be one of freely exercised care. He also contrasts the destructive certainty of his active addiction with the openness he encountered in poetry. This supplies a qualified example of interpretation becoming a historically productive or closing relation to the world. An account of what one is owed can organize the person's actual pursuit of an object; an encounter with cultural work can make that account less sovereign. Neither addiction nor certainty is universally paired, and no religious or epistemic trait may be inferred from either. The historical-commercial problem in §0.16 is sharpened: where public success has changed the conditions under which an activity remains personally compelling, people can develop other ways of conducting life, including costly, self-destructive and generative practices. ###### 0.17.2. The vineyard and the impossibility of owning living meaning

Read Mark 12:1–12 with Isaiah 5:1–7, Song of Songs 8:6–12, Mark 10:17–31, Luke 12:22–34 and Exodus 3:7–15. These are an interpretive conjunction supplied by the user, not proof that Mark directly cites Song 8 or that all the texts have one original authorial theology. The ravens/lilies wording is Luke 12 (Matthew 6 has birds/lilies), not Mark. The theological register and the materialist psychological translation must remain distinct.

The contrast is not an abstract rejection of all possession. It distinguishes the commercially organized yield of the vineyard, the speaker's claim over herself or her vineyard, and the irreducibility of love to the purchase of access to it. The claim that one's own life is not another's property may require powers of materially effective self-disposition. Mark 10's rich man is loved before being confronted with divestment, distribution to the poor and following Jesus. The promised new kinship and provision coexist with persecution. Luke's ravens and lilies challenge anxious accumulation and occur in a sequence including the rich fool, provision, almsgiving and treasure. The actual insecurity of poor people is not refuted by the text, nor is lack of provisioning a virtue. These passages give theological form to a different way of organizing the conditions of continued life, in which provision and participation need not be guaranteed by private accumulation or command over other lives.

Exodus 3:14, ehyeh asher ehyeh, is rendered in several ways including I am what I am and I will be what I will be. The immediate context includes God's hearing of enslaved people's cry, the promise of presence with Moses (3:12), and the divine name in 3:15. Reading this as a refusal of possessibility or exhaustive objectification is the user's theological interpretation, not a lexically compulsory meaning or a proof of metaphysical ontology. A theological Absolute Horizon can relativize every finite form, including the actor's own self and the institution through which the name became meaningful.

The materialist psychological successor is not an imperative to relinquish every possession. It is the distinction between having a real power of independent activity, possessing a thing, having an enforceable claim to another's labor or presence, and participating in a relation whose living reality depends on another being's independent activity. The attempt to secure what has become vital can take a possessive course through historically acquired assumptions of entitlement, available legal/material powers, insecurity and a particular relation to the self. It can also take a cooperative course through provision, protection of independence, negotiated commitments, and support of actual powers. Actual security can increase freedom. Actual domination can produce outwardly reliable participation while transforming the desired relation into something else. The other being's refusal is not a failure of the actor's developmental process, and no history guarantees another's love. The Absolute Horizon can render an acquired relation of appropriation visible and contestable from within the very good it was supposed to sustain; it can also make that appropriation sacred. This is a generative historical possibility, not a normative winning interpretation installed in every actor.

###### 0.17.3. The substrate supplies possible interpretations before they become sentences

The desired model must explain the production of possible sense, not merely rank explicit interpretations. A presently encountered sign is received in a historically organized body engaged in ongoing activities within independent material/social conditions. Its relevance is constituted through the intersection of actual practical conditions, available cultural forms, acquired distinctions, anticipations, relation histories, bodily condition, and the objects through which activity already has significance. These conditions do not independently vote for a conclusion. Each can transform the significance of another. A refusal from a trusted authority, a refusal by a stranger, a refusal when material livelihood depends on compliance, a refusal in a new context in which another source of support has become available, are not merely different weights on a fixed stimulus. What the refusal can *be for the person* changes.

The inherited intellectual handles are more precise than a generic appraisal theory. We can formulate the positive operation as historically constrained generation of practical significance. A candidate interpretation is a possible relation among (a) some personally available aspect of an actual or anticipated situation, (b) the person's operative historical organization and (c) what that conjunction makes relevant, possible, necessary, compelling, forbidden, threatening or unimportant. This is a conceptual relation, not a new mandatory record schema. Much of it can be nonverbal. The same occurrence can acquire incompatible senses, none of which becomes sovereign; another can have no significance beyond the immediate practical course. Some senses can be readily generated through an already acquired practice; others require a new composition of historically developed meanings; still others cannot be generated from the person's present powers and accessible conditions. Do not equate the latter with the model's inability to produce a plausible answer.

Consider a caregiver's failure to answer a child's attempt to share an accomplishment. The child may have an actual acquired expectation of responsive shared activity; a long history of being rewarded for quietness; a presently operative exploration of an object; bodily fatigue; and other accessible means. The child need not possess a proposition about rejection. Which distinctions are available is historical. The actual cause of the caregiver's conduct is not necessarily known to the child; the caregiver's own activity remains independently organized. A new consequence may change the meaning of the child's initial attempt. In neither case should the system select a surface response and then invent an antecedent interpretation to rationalize it.

This is a double temporality. Before settlement, the historically constituted person produces possible orientations, practical strategies and anticipated significance, which may organize conduct. During settlement, actual worldly/Other outcomes alter the field. In the afterlife, the lived consequence may retain, differentiate, contradict, or newly compose acquired organization. The tip may articulate what is now available to the actor; the articulation itself can become a new historical event through reflection, expression and self-reception. ##### 1. What flows is not pressure: the living historical production of channels

The early demand was for an intelligible unity beneath the person's descriptions of themself: an organization continuously shaped by its world, capable of transmitting a change in one sphere into a profound alteration of another. It resisted both the static trait and the episodic trigger. Its strongest proposition was not that every person has the same amount of psychic fluid, but that the changing organization of a life is one historically continuous reality. Its weakness was the temptation to make this unity a substance, and to give causal priority to pressure, quantity or an already named direction of striving.

PRE-A0 makes the decisive ontological correction: before pressure there is living movement. A body reaches, vocalizes, looks, rests, enjoys, explores, seeks contact and takes up objects. But even this must not be pictured as an autonomous stock of conation subsequently encountering a social world. The particular living movement is already a movement of a body sustained and differentiated through real relations. A newborn's ability to continue exploring depends upon temperature, nutrition, positioning, maturation, another person's availability and the material organization of that availability. Its directional movements are not yet necessarily desires for conceptually represented objects. The social and the organismic are distinguishable without being successive ontological layers.

**Pressure-flow names the historically developing organization of living activity through relations that activity itself can reproduce and transform.** It includes the production of new movements and new objects of activity, not merely the distribution of an antecedent force. What is preserved from the original intuition of vitality is the open capacity of an embodied life to become differently active. Vitality may expand, diminish, become selectively inaccessible, be organized into an intensive practice, or find a previously unavailable expression. It is not a psychometric total; actual time, bodily reserves and material resources remain real and may have domain-specific quantities. A finite hour can be spent only once. This does not imply that the enjoyment of a practice, the acquired capacity to perform it, its meaning to another person and its contribution to a political formation are portions of a fixed conserved sum.

A **channel** is a situated, historically constituted relation of effective possibility: a living actor with particular capabilities and organization can engage an object, person or process through particular bodily, practical, symbolic and material means. A channel can depend on another's assistance, on an institution's resources, on a practice the actor has acquired, or on the conjunction of these. It can be objectively available without being apprehended; apprehended without being wanted; wanted without being executable; exercised without being experienced as the actor expected. It can later become a means to something that was not an object of the original activity. The channel is not necessarily a new stored entity; it may be realized across the existing owners of these relations.

**Conductance** is the historically and situationally particular effectiveness of such mediation. It may concern accessibility, familiarity, skill, cost, habitual recruitment, the willingness of another, or the practical security needed to sustain an activity. These are not interchangeable quantities. A practice may be easily recruited but materially impossible today; an institution may provide an effective means while making its use humiliating; an intensely desired relation may be difficult to act within precisely because it matters. A change of conductance must therefore say what relation became different and what difference this can make, rather than increase a global coefficient.

A **groove** is recurrence that has become constitutive: an acquired route through which later activity can be organized without having to reconstruct the original occasion or consciously recollect it. Recurrence alone does not establish a groove. If repeated episodes remain individually stored but contribute nothing to the next actual recruitment, their historical existence has been conserved without that further realization. Conversely, an unpractised possibility can persist without becoming an operative habit. A historically active groove can be interrupted, transformed in function, left dormant or reactivated without erasing its origin.

A **wall** names heterogeneous forms of obstruction which must retain their causal distinction. A withheld resource is not lack of bodily capacity; lack of access to another's action is not a defensive inhibition; a competing undertaking is not a coercive prohibition; a person's actual refusal is not the same as a blocked wish. One wall can be constitutive of protection in another relation: saying no to an employer's demand can preserve time for care, if the worker can bear or collectively alter the consequences. A barrier can remain real after the actor ceases to desire what lies beyond it. There need not be energy waiting to discharge elsewhere. The historical pressure vocabulary survives by being grounded in the specific relations through which living activity becomes possible, impossible, costly or otherwise organized.

Pressure in the structural register names independently real forces and terms of life, including enabling and coercive ones, whether or not an actor attends to them. Pressure in the experiential/psychodynamic register concerns the particular encounter of movement, obligation, expectation, capacity and these conditions. They are not the same data. Neither an unmet possibility nor an external constraint automatically produces a consciously felt burden. The general response vocabulary BUFFER, AMPLIFY, DEFLECT, REROUTE, TRANSFORM and BLOCK can discriminate the differences a mediation makes, but does not establish a universal developmental law. A present response and its durable afterlife are separate causal questions. A transformation can generate more possible movement rather than merely redirect something that was already there.

This corrects both the earliest hydraulic imagination and its possible overcorrection into a graph of semantic relations. The unity of the person is neither a substance nor a diagram. It is the same materially and historically continuing life whose acquired organizations make differences to its subsequent activity. The representations are only adequate insofar as they participate in that continuity.

##### 2. The germ: a child and caregiver produce a capacity neither owns alone in advance

Consider a very young child making exploratory movements toward an object. The child's body can do certain things and not others. The object, its position and the child's posture are independently real conditions. A caregiver brings it within reach, supports the child's body, imitates a sound, or leaves space for another attempt. This is the caregiver's activity, with a particular orientation of their own: perhaps delight in the child's developing ability, perhaps sustaining care under fatigue, perhaps fulfilling an obligation, perhaps asserting control. No adult interpretation is projected into the child.

The child's actual movement matters. The caregiver has not performed the child's achievement on its behalf merely by making it possible. Nor does the child's success imply that it could have occurred without the caregiver. At this stage an effective channel exists **in the conjunction of two independently continuing lives and a materially arranged situation**. Its causal conditions are distributed. Its outcomes are not.

The child can come to differentiate a movement that has effects from one that does not. A contingent, repeatable experience of effect can help produce a new sensorimotor coordination. The caregiver may respond with attention, pleasure, a smile or shared vocalization; if receivable, that action can itself become part of the child's experience. The child's own activity can become pleasurable, the caregiver's response can become anticipated, their conjunction can become meaningful, and an existing pattern of mutual attending can change. These are distinct possibilities. The child need not yet possess the concepts of recognition, ability, love or self-worth to undergo such differentiation. What later becomes conceptually articulate can develop from material and relational activity without being a hidden adult interpretation at birth.

Recognition here means more than reward: another person's actual activity can make the child's achievement socially real, and the child may undergo the other's recognition in a developmentally available form. Recognition is not simply approval, nor does it reduce to a pleasurable sensation. A hostile witness can also make an act socially consequential. A child may enjoy an activity without a witness; a caregiver can celebrate an event the child did not accomplish; a child may not receive the celebration; both may participate without experiencing the same pleasure. The child's success, the adult's recognition, the child's reception, and any durable change each require their own causal account.

Over a history of suitable activity, the channel may become partly appropriated by the child. The child can now perform the previously assisted operation with less assistance, or initiate it without the former invitation, or use it in relation to a different object or another person. We must distinguish actual acquired capability from the success of one assisted episode. What has developed is not a weight assigned to a goal, but a practical power that changes what the child can do. In some cases this further makes a new object of activity possible: the child can explore for the pleasure of exploring, communicate with a sibling, make something for another, continue a game not initiated by the caregiver. The content of future desire is not limited to more frequent pursuit of an initial reward.

The reciprocal consequence is fundamental. The caregiver's earlier activity has helped produce someone who can now act without that same mediation. A project can succeed by transforming or ending the practical relation through which it was accomplished. The caregiver may delight in the child's independence, experience a loss of their former place, feel relief from work, redirect attention to something else, intensify control, or recognize an altogether new relation to the child. More than one of these can coexist; none follows automatically. The child's independently acquired means is not evidence that it no longer loves or seeks the caregiver, or that the caregiver's response is shared by the child. A more differentiated relation becomes possible because an earlier form of dependence has actually changed. The possibility of mature mutuality is produced by that differentiation, not guaranteed by it.

This is the profound truth of the user's November 19 desiring-machine articulation. A connection has produced the conditions of its own alteration. The relation has participated in the genesis of something that can no longer be identified with it. There can be a positive contradiction between the caregiver's successful activity and the continuation of the particular channel through which the caregiver had been necessary. Loss, renewed intimacy and freedom are not mutually exclusive. The same child can still need care in other respects, and the acquired capability can make new interdependencies possible. Practical independence is never a passage out of material and relational life.

There are equally constitutive counterhistories. Assistance may replace the child's own movement; the child may resist an imposed activity; the adult's pleasure may concern the performance of an idealized parental identity rather than the child's expanding life; a new capacity may threaten the adult's control; fatigue, hunger or unavailable equipment may prevent development despite loving invitation. Even effective scaffolding may yield no durable change. Independence in one practice can increase dependence in another. Several caregivers can offer differing conditions, and the relations among them can organize what any one of them can offer. The child encounters peers, objects and institutions that neither parent controls. The same theory must be able to generate these divergent histories without forcing them into an infantile pathology of separation, a narrative of fused wills, an attachment score or a universal reinforcement curve.

The caregiver is not a developmental service attached to the child. Their life is being organized through work, material obligation, bodily condition, love, prior acquisitions, expectations and their other relationships. Care makes new lives possible because it consumes and mobilizes real resources and capacities. To model the child's achievement without the caregiver's reproductive labor would abstract the productive relation from its material conditions. To give the adult's desire automatic priority would mistake power over the situation for ownership of the child's desire. The same reciprocal architecture that will later organize friendship, teaching, cooperation, exploitation and political activity begins here, in a developmentally sparse form.

##### 3. Desire produces, activity specifies, personal sense differentiates

The Deleuzian intuition is indispensable insofar as it refuses to treat desire exclusively as a lack seeking an already defined object. Desiring-production names the productivity of living connections: their capacity to make new forms of activity possible, produce new objects and practices, and transform the connections themselves. It does not imply that every connection is liberating, every experience formative, or every obstruction creative. A connection can reproduce domination, and a productive capacity can make its possessor more exploitable. Desiring-machines are a theoretical orientation for constructing the organism, not a new metaphysical substance or a mandatory software class.

Leontiev gives this productivity a more exact psychological articulation. The activity in which an action participates, its object/motive, the action's more particular goal and the means or operation by which it is accomplished are not identical. A person can learn to carry out an operation through one activity and subsequently employ it in another. The sense of an action for a person arises from its relation to their life and its motivating activities, not solely from what the action does technically. This does not require all action to be mediated by verbally formulated motives; infant exploratory movement and acquired preconceptual practical differentiation must not be made miniature versions of adult projects.

Suppose the child later helps a peer reach an object, reproducing part of a caregiver's earlier mode of assistance. The child may be exercising a new capability, enjoying a shared game, responding to the peer's difficulty, seeking the adult's recognition, or participating in several of these differently organized movements. It may not possess all of these motives, and the meanings need not be accessible to it. The other child may refuse the assistance. Successful performance of the operation is no guarantee of the fulfillment of the socially significant activity. If the child acquires a way of helping that works, that acquired means may become an independently valued activity; alternatively it can become a means of controlling others or establishing conditional worth. Which development occurs is a historical question, not a rule that a capacity has one psychologically correct destination.

An achievement can even contradict its own motivating relation. The practical success of an action undertaken partly to be seen by another may fail to realize the wished-for mutuality; an action can satisfy an assigned task while undermining the actor's relation to its moral or practical object. This is the constitutive force of the bitter-candy problem. A single event can have several real but non-equivalent consequences for one person, as well as different consequences for each participant. Affect can express a historically specific relation between these consequences without becoming a complete report on their causes. Neither the child's expression nor the adult's interpretation of it settles what happened to the child.

Consequently, simultaneous activity is not adequately modeled as a competition among pre-existing named projects for one winning output. Several organizations may be operative in recruitment, attentional differentiation, bodily preparedness, inhibition, the form and timing of expression, and the practical conduct that follows. One may inhibit one particular manifestation of another while making a third activity more possible; one may supply the means by which two distinct motives are jointly enacted; several may remain unresolved. An actual selected action, an inhibited course, a consciously deferred undertaking and a merely available unchosen alternative have different evidentiary status. Their absence from the outward act is not evidence that they were all active or all absent. No compulsory winner, scalar cancellation, invented opportunity loss or compulsory synthesis is warranted.

This is how the variable ontology of SEAM/21 becomes more than a larger vocabulary. An acquired organization can acquire a different **function** within a new relation without changing all its contents or becoming a new species of psychological thing. A previously acquired capacity, expectation, defense or pleasure can participate in differing activities under historically specific conditions. The organism can acquire a new object of desire, a new way to pursue an old object, or a different relation to the object itself. The emergence of these possibilities is what a generative psychological machine must help realize.

##### 4. The exercise–parental recognition counterhistory: how a new channel changes an old one

The original pressure-flow discussion's exercise example is a particularly demanding test of the theory. Imagine a person whose history with a parent has organized the parent's judgment as an unusually consequential condition of self-worth and whose body and practical life have become implicated in that relation. It is insufficient to simulate exercise and increment confidence, decrement approval-seeking, or make the person articulate a healthier belief. Those changes presume the causal answer.

Suppose instead that physical practice becomes genuinely accessible, is undertaken repeatedly and generates actual experienced differences: a movement becomes possible, exertion can be sustained, a bodily condition changes, the activity becomes interesting in itself. Perhaps others become available who can witness the person's achievements without occupying the parent's historically invested position. Perhaps the person can continue the practice without obtaining the parent's approval. A new practical channel and a new set of relations can now constitute a different effective field of life. These changes can supply lived evidence that competence, pleasure or continuation are possible through means other than the old recognition relation. They can also fail to do so.

Where the historically acquired organization had linked the possibility of being worthwhile or continuing an undertaking to a parent's judgment, activity that acquires independent sense and effective means can alter the function of the old relation. The parent need not first apologize, understand the person, or become less demanding. The same criticism can still be received, still hurt, and no longer organize the same subsequent conduct. The person might cease an earlier appeasement, maintain a practice despite disapproval, or become able to expose a previously concealed weakness without that disclosure organizing the whole relation. None requires the person to reconstruct the causal history or declare a new self-concept.

The parent now encounters a different actual action. That action can transform what the parent can do, elicit new attempts at control, enable recognition of a previously unavailable relation, or change nothing in the parent. The alteration of the actor's psychic organization becomes causally productive in the independently real relationship; what the relationship subsequently becomes can affect both participants again. Thus the initial exercise channel has not simply displaced a quantity of pressure. Through the production of a capacity and its historical organization, it has helped change the conditions under which another movement acquires its meaning and efficacy.

This is not a therapeutic law. The same exercise can become a refined instrument of parental appeasement or socially imposed bodily discipline; a new coach can occupy an analogous position of conditional approval; a gain in bodily competence can coexist with greater insecurity in the old relationship. The actor may remain materially dependent on the parent, unable to act on a changed valuation; the cost of an apparently small refusal can be very high. Or nothing about the prior relation may change. Compare histories in which an apparently identical technical achievement arises through different actual activities, receives different recognition, and changes or fails to change an established relation. The causal difference belongs to the history of their conjunction, not a universal transfer from exercise to self-esteem.

The old V3 account of cross-domain movement, conductance and changed channels therefore survives in a stronger form: a local practical acquisition can alter the organization through which an independently constituted relation becomes operative. This requires no universal currency for translating fitness into confidence or confidence into affection. What has changed is a historically evidenced relation of dependence, meaning, practical possibility, recruitment or protection. The transformation can itself become a condition of later development. That is the phenomenon the builders should realize, rather than only another interactivity edge between named projects.

##### 5. The Personal Relational Field is a historical topology of efficacy, not a relational picture of a self

##### 7. The Absolute Horizon: when a historically produced good exceeds its producing relation

Return to the child and caregiver. The caregiver may act in the service of a good the child later comes to participate in: care, competence, fairness, fidelity, the possibility of another person's flourishing. This is not the same as transmitting a fully articulated moral principle. The good can become real in the child's own activity through what it helps make possible, its consequences for others, and the changing meanings the child develops. Its initial material and relational mediation is indispensable. It need not remain the source of its authority for the person.

As the person's activity expands, an acquired good can acquire authority *against the authority through which it first became available*. A person who has learned the significance of caring for a vulnerable other in a family or religious community may encounter the same relation of vulnerability where that community withholds care. Their existing organization may make the exclusion intelligible as a contradiction; another actual practice and relation may make responding possible. They might begin to treat the good as authoritative beyond the finite carrier, without having to cease loving or depending on everyone within it. They might instead preserve the carrier's authority by qualifying the good, reject both, conceal a contrary practice, or organize their identity around opposition to the old carrier so completely that it remains their negative center.

The Absolute Horizon names the possibility that the historically acquired good becomes a principle of organization not exhausted by any one finite stratum or mediating authority. This is not a tenth structural stratum, a transcendent causal entity, a religion score, an innate moral axis or a developmental destination. In a historical-material computational account it is the changing relation between particular acquired goods, finite authorities and their independently encountered consequences. A theological or secular articulation may become part of that history; it is not the engine's metaphysical verdict. The early Horizon conception of relativization without disembedding is decisive: critical fidelity does not take the person outside material dependence, institutional power or embodied limits.

Its actual operation is more demanding than an avowed value winning a motive conflict. The actor must have materially practicable means to sustain the good in a changed relation. A person may refuse an institutional demand and yet reproduce the institution through another activity. A caregiver may defend the child's autonomy in one domain and require the child to sustain their own image of good parenthood in another. A person may acquire greater structural clarity while becoming less solidaristic, or develop practical solidarity without possessing that clarity. Conversely, the pleasure of collective understanding and achievement can become a new channel of vitality; structural thought is not necessarily misery or an analyst's intrusive overlay. The original desire for nontransparent determination must not become a ban on historically earned understanding.

Pressure-flow here includes the historical transformation of the authority by which activity is organized. A formerly subordinate good can come to qualify or reorganize an inherited relation, including the circumstances in which it is recruited and sustained. There is no necessary redemption, final coherence, dehumanizing type or prescribed relation between suffering and liberation. Reified relational forms, such as the image of the good parent or the sanctification of an institution, may become objects of preservation even at the expense of the living relations they claim to embody. A living good can also acquire new practical forms through which a person changes actual reproductive conditions for others. That is the stronger potential of Horizon: not a moral sorting mechanism but the development of powers and historically real commitments through which existing authorities may become contestable.
