**The object.** The theory concerns a historically particular, biologically maturing, relationally formed person acting among independent people and material institutions. It concerns not a list of personality traits but the production, reproduction, operation, inhibition, reorganization and possible nonreorganization of the means by which that person can live. The psyche is contradictory, partly inaccessible even to its bearer, sometimes coherently settled, capable of acquiring ways of knowing, wanting, acting and understanding itself that did not previously exist. The person is neither an internally self-sufficient will nor a reflection dictated by external structure. What she can do is materially conditioned; what she imagines, learns and makes of it has its own particular, historically derived forms. No desirable adult endpoint, trauma arc, emancipatory destiny, romantic closure or universally valid motive menu is presupposed.

**The governing explanatory movement.** Previously acquired bodily and psychological organizations condition what is operative now. The person's actual activity, through available means and alongside independent Others, alters or fails to alter material circumstances. Different participants encounter different consequences, interpret or misinterpret them, and carry distinct afterlives into further life. This movement is not a sequence of compulsory ticks. A life can continue in routine, overlapping activities without a salient event; a new end can arise before a visitor or narrator supplies a prompt; no durable psychic change need follow even a consequential occurrence. Recruitment is the present historical efficacy of acquisition; genesis is the possible formation of later efficacy through present lived activity. Both take place within independent economic, social, ecological and institutional reality.

**What counts as a successful theory.** It must explain more than a psychologically persuasive description. It must distinguish bodily condition from a need's historically found object; objective opportunity from recognized or imagined possibility; action from intention; apparent self-story from practical organization; actual event from memory; inward formation from public expression; shared relation from a shared psyche; structure from the actor's reason; the same source distribution from an individual's unknown history. It must preserve negative cases: ability without opportunity, pain without learning, love without truth, closeness without access, ritual without belief, speech without self-knowledge, practice without new motive, contradiction without dialectical reconciliation, and a consequential world event without the developmental arc the analyst expected. The stronger theory must *change the explanatory relation among these* rather than simply mention them all.

**The epistemic status of the manuscript.** Leontiev, Lahire, Sève, Dreier, Luria, Holzkamp and Archer provide partly complementary and partly competing theories. The inherited PFF, SDTF, CAN/ADP, V3, PRE-A0 and SOROK source work supplies a historically layered functional architecture and experiments of varied authority. None is transmuted into a general empirically established law by literary integration. Scientific and materialist psychology can identify mechanisms and conditional regularities while remaining unable to reconstruct the uniquely actual motive of an arbitrary unobserved human respondent. Particular history is not a black box filled by an LLM; neither is it an extra metaphysical why in need of a universal selector.

## I. A person is not a latent template awaiting events

### 1. The living unit: a history that conducts several activities at once

To understand Nadia on an ordinary morning, we cannot begin by asking which preset psychological trait a stimulus activates. Before anybody enters the room, she is conducting a life. Her body bears yesterday's sleep, a learned gait, a sensory repertoire, present discomfort, capacities acquired over years and limitations that may have nothing to do with her current concerns. She has work to do, promises to keep, people to whom she is accountable, desires that do not coincide, and possible futures whose meaning may be ambiguous even to her. A familiar object can be passed without notice; a distant commitment can organize attention while she speaks politely about something else. This is activity, not merely a bag of event responses.

A life does not have one active project at a time. Driving while rehearsing a conversation, completing a task while resenting a workplace hierarchy, caring for a child while imagining the quiet of an empty house, or attending to a conversation while monitoring the body for discomfort are not serial personality switches. Different activities possess different objects, means, histories, obligations, feasible time horizons, embodied rhythms and personal senses. Their present relation may be supportive, inhibiting, contradictory, mutually indifferent or entirely unrecognized. An outwardly competent action can proceed through a practiced operation while another activity becomes emotionally dominant; the reverse is equally possible. We must explain which differentially acquired organizations are implicated, not announce that their numeric weights have been added.

The supposed hidden central controller is also not a free-standing soul of coordination. The person can acquire methods of self-direction: waiting, comparing alternatives, giving oneself a reminder, silently rehearsing a phrase, learning to endure uncertainty, avoiding an image, taking another's perspective or exposing one's reason to questioning. These methods themselves have histories, material affordances and cultural forms. Some people use extended verbal internal discussion; others rely more upon bodily routine, imagery, practice, social consultation or forms of attention not describable as an adult deliberative monologue. The absence of a particular reflective practice does not make an infant, disabled adult, illiterate worker or other culturally situated person less psychological.

This means that an actual present response is a *historically situated production*. It can be stable, ordinary, surprising, improvised, half willed, conflicted, misdirected or unremarkable. It need not transparently express the deepest motive. It need not produce a new trait. The actor may subsequently notice what they just did and reconsider it, or never do so. What the analyst calls a moment is only one cut through continuing relations.

### 2. The independent world and the nonidentity of its determinations

This person inhabits an objective material world. A locked workshop, an unpaid rent bill, an exhausted supply of medicine, the withdrawal of state support, the departure of a friend, a broken instrument and the existence of a library are not psychological events just because they become meaningful to someone. They change what is available and costly even where the person fails to perceive the change. An institution can enforce a consequence by law or wage relation while its subject offers a sincere but false description of why an action is impossible. Another person's decision can alter the situation without being motivated by the focal person at all.

Equally, the objective situation does not mechanically provide an interpretation. The same classroom can distribute literacy, humiliation, curiosity, mutual help and a fear of failure through materially different opportunities and actual participations. Two siblings can inhabit one household without inhabiting one history; a third person's labor can enable care while remaining largely unknown to the recipient. Even seemingly equal resources do not erase differences in bodily condition, obligations, acquired means or the consequences of particular encounters. Historical materialism here names the necessity of explaining the means, powers, institutions and reproduced conditions of life, not a license to derive an individual's precise fantasy from a class label.

The causal status of a condition must therefore be identified separately from its representation. What an actor can do, what she knows she can do, what she believes she can do, what she would attempt, what the world would permit, what would result, and what she might later make of that result are not seven verbal views of one hidden score. The relation among them is historically formed and transformable. A physical disability is not exhausted by a social prohibition; social exclusions are not rendered bodily inevitabilities by being attached to a body. An effective power can be misunderstood yet effective. A false hope can motivate an actual attempt. An unexpected result can establish an opportunity that no one anticipated.

### 3. Contingency and particularity answer the wrongly posed selection problem

Why did this person learn to want a particular object? The meaningful answer reconstructs a situated genealogy: a bodily and material situation, an acquired way of recognizing an affordance, a remembered or unremembered practice, an encounter with someone else's activity, an anticipated consequence, a material possibility or impossibility, and whatever subsequent participation made the object worth returning to—or prevented that transformation. Such explanations may be incomplete; causal roles may be disputed. But a demand for a single deeper law selecting every individual's exact destiny from every possible biography mistakes a contingent historical world for a system with a guaranteed abstract solution.

The right distinctions are genealogical explanation, contrastive explanation and distributional explanation. The first asks how this actual end became possible and operative for this historically particular actor. The second compares histories to determine what changed the outcome. The third studies which processes recur under relevantly comparable material and social conditions, and with what variability. These are compatible with contingency. They do not require that two people ever occupy the same total developmental state or encounter identical Others. Whether the physical world is ultimately deterministic or partly irreducibly stochastic is not decided by this theory, and no imaginary god-resolution camera could substitute for a discriminating causal account.

Particularity is not an alibi for psychologizing by fiat. “Her unique history did it” explains nothing until that history has identifiable causal mediation. Nor may a model fill in whatever childhood event seems convenient after it observes a preferred adult outcome. The open future of Nadia differs fundamentally from a retrospectively constructed adult hypothesis. The latter is constrained by empirical evidence and by any established fictional facts; the former must arise through genuinely ensuing events and activity. In neither case does a plausible narrative become proven knowledge of a real survey respondent's hidden life.

### 4. Unity is continuity, not harmony

A person's earlier practical learning can transform what another acquired relation does, and two contradictory relations can become a new way of acting. But this is only one possible kind of development. The same person can sustain an ethical commitment in one setting while exploiting someone in another; a childhood affection can remain strong without being reconciled to later contempt; a capacity may never be exercised; two histories may coexist without ever interacting. A later encounter may reinterpret a past event without changing what happened. An inner conflict may be painful and sterile. A person may be unreflectively at ease. Neither a jaggedly conflicted life nor a coherently ordinary one is more human by definition.

The unity that matters is that these parts belong to one actually continuous, bodily and historically located person who must conduct this life through time. That person cannot be composed from a universal profile on every occasion without losing the historical causality of acquired practices. Nor can the model consolidate every contradictory current into one superior self-concept. It must carry what remains apart, what has become related, and what is still unknown, each with its own kind of evidence. This is why the theory cannot be completed by deciding what Nadia ought to become.

**First adversarial contrast.** Imagine two adults subjected to comparable work demands and equally able to describe their exploitation. One gradually takes up collective action; another narrows attention toward keeping the household intact; a third withdraws from public life. The theory must identify the unequal lived histories, relationships, bodily conditions, resources, opportunities and practical premises through which each course became available, not infer three inborn moral types from a shared class position. And it must preserve a fourth case in which the person remains substantially as before. If all four are explained by attaching the same vague word “contingency,” the explanation has failed.

## II. A body becomes psychological through activity, care and cultural means

### 1. Need is real before it has an acquired object

An organism begins with bodily requirements and capacities, not with a finished inventory of what these requirements will mean. Hunger does not arrive knowing which food, sharing practice, ritual, prohibition or relation to a feeder will become its object. Vulnerability, fatigue, pain, arousal, startle, sensory pleasure, movement and regulation are bodily realities before the child can describe them. They are also encountered in already historical ecologies. Who provides relief, with what rhythm, in what dwelling, through whose labor, under which constraints, and with what repeated gestures and signs can transform the child's anticipation and way of acting.

The distinction between organismic need and the historically objectified motive is indispensable. A child may reach for a familiar person because that person's actual practices have become associated with safety, not because nature installed that particular person as an abstract attachment endpoint. The caregiver may be anxious, loving, obligated, resentful, exhausted or distracted; the child is not born knowing those reasons. The adult's achieved activity and the child's reception form separate histories. Care can be nourishing without being equally experienced by both; repeated care can establish tacit reliance without explicit autobiographical memory or verbal knowledge.

There is no reason to require every need to become a conscious desire or every strong feeling to signal what is most important to a person. Immediate physiological relief can be intensely felt without organizing a life. A meaning-forming motive can persist through unpleasant acts and even suppress a gratifying impulse. An object can be materially available but unrecognized, recognized but unwanted, wanted but infeasible, pursued without lasting significance, or made valuable only after doing something with it. Body, object, personal sense, affect, incentive and durable purpose are not interchangeable variables.

### 2. The means of knowing are acquired, not one universal menu

Development changes not only the contents of perception but the forms of perception and action available. A child learns to handle a spoon through particular feeding practices, to make an unfamiliar sound through repeated coordination with another body, to follow a route through embodied familiarity, to recognize a tool through activity. Language can make absent events jointly discussable, but languages, signed practices, writing, ritual, stories, work apprenticeship and spatial organization do not all form the same mental operations with differing efficiency. What counts as a useful category, a reason, a memory or a possible self is itself historically variable.

A literate schoolchild may learn by explicit verbal analysis; a person apprenticed through observation and repeated coactivity may develop fine practical discrimination without an accompanying school-style explanation. A native signer does not possess a lesser representation because their language is embodied differently. A child whose environment lacks accessible language opportunities may be deprived of particular cultural means without being an empty organism. Disability can alter access to a practice; social organization can magnify or mitigate that alteration. To assume one standard adult internal conversation and measure everyone else's distance from it would reproduce the hierarchy the theory is supposed to explain materially.

The theory consequently needs developmental *kinds* rather than an all-purpose scale of cognitive sophistication. An acquired object permanence practice, an analogical use of a tool, an auditory anticipation, a public rule, an internalized evaluative phrase and a practiced mutual rhythmic adjustment do not have one identical form just because they can all affect later activity. Their traces may be bodily, linguistic, practical, symbolic, imagistic or inseparably combined. We should not infer their existence from a narrator's use of an evocative word; we should know what history and performance made the claim plausible.

### 3. Maturation and sociogenesis alter each other without becoming synonyms

Biological maturation establishes conditions and limits; it does not single-handedly produce the social organization that will be developed. A capacity to move and a socially available practice of moving are different, just as the ability to make vocal sound is not a language system. Some processes depend on the changing body; others require sustained access to other people, objects, practical demonstrations or symbolic means. Maturation may open a new possibility while the material world keeps it unavailable. Conversely a social practice may reorganize attention and control within existing bodily limits. Developmental explanations need to specify which relation is doing what.

Nadia's early development cannot be written backwards from a final adult profile. She might become musically attentive through a caregiver's singing, develop a different kind of auditory interest while repairing an instrument, or take little interest despite repeated lessons. At one age she may enjoy the sound, later anticipate a turn in shared song, later repeat a pattern alone, later invent a variant, later reinterpret its association with her mother. None of these steps is identical with a durable musical identity. The mother may have provided social access without intending a future artist; the jointly produced sound may be genuinely new for both. The child may retain the learned capacity while distancing herself from the relationship that supplied it.

**Second adversarial contrast.** Two children have access to similar songs and caring adults. One becomes sensitive to rhythm but refuses public performance. The other enjoys performance without seeking solitary musical practice. Both can remain perfectly ordinary adults; neither requires a hidden lost vocation. A third child becomes a different musician by connecting a sound to a local work practice, an unexpected friendship or an available device. The important differences are accessible forms of practice, personally felt result, material possibility, acquired self-direction and time. The theory cannot infer an artist's essence from exposure, or guarantee that all apparently different trajectories hide the same latent potential.

### 4. Social reproduction precedes and exceeds the child's understanding

A meal may be made possible by wages earned elsewhere, care work performed without pay, property relations governing who owns the kitchen, labor incorporated into appliances and food, transport systems, ecological production and local forms of obligation. The child ordinarily does not experience those entire conditions as a concept. The conditions nevertheless shape the regularity, cost, exhaustion, availability and personal form of feeding. A parent may experience care as freely chosen and yet be financially compelled to organize it in a particular way. Another may love the child and resent the distribution of work. These objective and subjective facts may coexist without cancelling one another.

This is why the relationship between historical materialism and developmental psychology cannot be reduced to “external social causes plus internal interpretation.” Material arrangements produce the means of activity, shape relations among the people who perform it, distribute the time for attention and learning, and make some consequences durable across contexts. The child actively appropriates, ignores, misrecognizes or transforms what becomes available, but does not choose the conditions under which appropriation is possible. Similarly, adult knowledge of social structure does not instantly undo the practical and affective history of living under it.

**Third adversarial contrast.** One child learns competence by helping a parent perform necessary work; another acquires the same competence through humiliating discipline; a third has the capacity but is not allowed to exercise it. The model must distinguish the usable skill from acquired self-understanding, the caregiver's intent from the child's history, and both from the material division of labor that made the practice necessary. Predicting the same psyche because the displayed “skill acquired” flag matches would violate the explanatory object.

## III. The possible is materially produced: power, capacities and available time

### 1. Practical ability is a relation, not a property of the solitary individual

A person does not own practical ability in the way a box contains an item. An acquired capability exists in a bodily and social history, but its exercise needs materials, time, permission, opportunity and frequently another person's cooperation. A woman may know how to repair machinery while having no access to the necessary tools; a musician may retain an entire repertoire while working two shifts; a parent may know how to comfort a child and still be physically absent because paid work determines the hours. These are not contradictory descriptions of one skill. They distinguish a developed capacity, an actual opportunity and the historically organized use of time.

The same distinction is necessary for apparent liberty. An unemployed person may be formally free to apply for jobs while lacking transportation, documents, child care or a safe route. A tenant may be able to imagine refusing a landlord's demand without possessing effective protection from eviction. A person can have a completely coherent wish and lack the means to undertake it. Others may control the keys, the money, the institutional clock, the legal category, the machinery and the authority to interpret a request. That control is effective whether or not the person has a correct theory of it. No amount of simulated self-confidence can alter an actual locked door.

Conversely, a materially effective act can arise without a fully conscious account of its social function. An employee might teach a new colleague a method, unintentionally undermine a hierarchy of workplace knowledge, or reproduce a humiliating protocol while sincerely thinking they are merely helping. Such consequences are not reducible to the employee's inner meaning. But they also do not automatically produce a new political commitment. World effects and personal uptake have different conditions.

### 2. The social organization of time constitutes the biography

An hour is not a neutral unit of psychological learning. One hour of recovery after night work, of involuntary waiting, of affectionate play, of domestic labor, of attentive apprenticeship, of terror or of private solitary practice does not produce the same possible development. Nor are these activities available equally. Ownership, employment, debt, caregiving arrangements, disability accommodations, classed expectations, public infrastructure and the independent schedules of Others allocate the times in which capacities can be exercised or suppressed. Sève's relation between acts, capacities and the social use of life-time is a serious proposal about the material constitution of personality, not a measured formula for converting hours into traits.

A person can develop considerable aptitude while being unable to make it socially consequential. Another can inherit institutional space to practice an unremarkable ability until it becomes recognized as talent. A person may spend an entire life refining a survival practice that brings little pleasure; another may repeatedly seek a pleasurable practice without becoming skilled at it. The model must register whether a possibility is bodily available, materially afforded, socially permitted, knowingly understood, actually exercised and sustained. It must never treat the existence of a capability as proof of the institutionally effective right to use it.

The time spent reproducing life cannot be called psychologically empty just because it is unspectacular. Cleaning a home, fetching water, preparing a meal, waiting for a bus, recovering from a night shift, keeping an appointment or ensuring that somebody else can sleep are real activities with costs, anticipations, social meanings and possible acquired traces. Sometimes they deepen familiarity, resilience, resentment, skill or attachment; sometimes they produce mere exhaustion or no meaningful new organization. A simulation that grants a large developmental dividend to every repeated task would be as false as one that treats routine as a blank screen.

### 3. Objectivity must resist the actor's available interpretation

Suppose Nadia loses access to a room because the property owner changes the locks. She may believe a friend betrayed her, suspect punishment for an argument, or think her own incompetence is to blame. The physical loss remains real; her premises may be wrong. If she acts against the friend, that act has its own independent material and relational consequences. Later discovering the property relation may change her understanding without undoing the harm of the accusation. This is one actual history with several different causal descriptions—legal, spatial, practical, affective, interpretive—and the model cannot choose the most emotionally satisfying one as the objective truth.

The converse also matters. A person may correctly understand a structural harm while remaining emotionally attached to the institution or person implicated. A worker can accurately identify exploitation and still value the craft, the coworkers, the security or the routine the employer makes accessible. A child can recognize abusive discipline and preserve affection for the teacher who supplied a powerful way of solving problems. A mother may believe a domestic arrangement is fair while carrying most of the reproductive labor. The structural analysis does not substitute for a theory of the actor's practical premises, just as subjective sincerity cannot negate the structure.

Holzkamp's actor-oriented vocabulary and the work of Leontiev, Lahire and Archer illuminate different aspects of this field: objective practical possibility, situated reasons, learned dispositions and the reflexive relation to concern. None should be silently collapsed into an invariant “belief about constraints.” The same actor may act from a reason she can articulate and a historical practice she cannot, under a restriction she misunderstands, in pursuit of a purpose that the world defeats. This causal complexity is not a computational luxury; it is what prevents a materially impossible act from succeeding merely because a narrator describes a sufficiently forceful intention.

### 4. Power is not the only process, but it remains independently effective

Materialist gravity does not mean every human event is a disguised class struggle. A child can play for pleasure, a couple can enjoy intimacy, an elder can attend to a bird, and a worker can become fascinated with the sound of a tool. Such experiences are not less real because they are not politically legible. Yet the time, safety, bodily access, objects and relations that make them possible belong to historically organized conditions. A person can invent a genuinely new purpose only within possibilities encountered through life, imagination and material means; those possibilities are themselves unequally distributed.

Power also operates through classification. Who can name another person mentally unfit, undeserving, beautiful, dangerous, respectable, employable or belonging? Who can attach a sanction or reward to the classification? Who gets heard as a reliable witness? These materially supported categories can become actor-owned ways of seeing, including when they are objectively false or injurious. A person may later use the same learned category against its source, or reproduce its violence in another relation. The same institution can produce competence and alienation without the psyche being obliged to resolve either into a single verdict.

Our central causal test is therefore not whether a biography contains politically recognizable terms. It is whether changing a real condition would change the actor's available actions and perhaps later development, while leaving room for distinct interpretation and null psychological uptake. If removing a tool, a right or a day's available time makes no difference to what the fictional person can materially accomplish, the world is scenery. If it automatically converts their consciousness into the analyst's preferred political conclusion, the psyche is scenery.

**Counterhistory: the four capacities.** Four adults know how to produce the same object. One has protected time and workshop access and develops a new practice. Another has the resources but no interest and does nothing. A third longs to work but cannot secure the means. A fourth is compelled to practice for an employer and becomes adept while resenting the work. Their capacities may look equivalent on a skills table. Their actual lives cannot be generated by the same “skill leads to motive” transition.

**Counterhistory: the structure without recognition.** Two tenants face an identical eviction notice. One understands the legal basis and organizes a response; another misreads it as a personal insult and damages a friendship; a third cannot respond because of illness; a fourth obtains an unforeseen remedy through someone else's independently initiated help. A theory that reduces them to different “agency” levels loses both the law and the history through which possible actions were arranged.

## IV. From possible object to acquired motive: historical novelty without a selector oracle

### 1. A new possibility has stages that must not be collapsed

There is a difference between coming across an object, attending to it, noticing what it might do, imagining using it differently, finding that use attractive, attempting it, receiving its consequences, repeating it, and developing a motive capable of reorganizing other activities. A full account of novelty must allow these movements to stop, branch, reverse, conceal themselves or occur in different historical orders. A new motive is not guaranteed merely because the model can phrase a compelling hypothetical desire. The possibility of an end is itself historically produced, but it does not arrive with proof that the person will care about it.

A young Nadia may watch a parent coordinate a rhythm. She can move in time before she has a musical concept, and enjoy a repeated sound before she seeks to make it again. At another age she might hear an unfamiliar interval and imagine what would happen if she altered an instrument. The imagined modification might never be attempted. She could be compelled to practice and acquire skill without wishing to continue; she could later find a wholly different social purpose for the skill. Each possibility has a materially different genealogy. When one new motive becomes durable, its durability is a fact about repeated, meaningful and feasible participation under this history—not an essence waiting in a hidden trait.

Leontiev's distinction among activity, action, object and motive bears on the transition whereby something initially done as a means can later become an end. But this does not authorize a universal frequency or direction of transformation. An act may repeatedly remain only an instrument. A task's unexpected result may attract attention without generating motivation. A personally significant end may be pursued through a hated action. The actor's body may provide immediate pleasure while the significance of the practice lies elsewhere. These differences have to be kept alive in the generated causal process.

### 2. Anticipation grows from capacities already acquired

The organism need not wait for a new external event to begin a new course. A person can attend to a memory, silently rehearse a possible conversation, mentally try an unfamiliar use of a familiar object, draw an analogy between two relations, or seek a particular encounter without a visitor-triggered prompt. But self-generated activity is not uncaused. It becomes possible through a previously acquired repertoire of perception, action, language, imagination, emotional association and socially available futures. A person unfamiliar with a practice cannot invent its entire operative cultural world by mere narrator fiat; they can conjecture, imitate, recombine, misapply or discover according to their actual means.

Imagining another possibility is different from performing it. It can influence later attention without creating an objective external event. A person who privately rehearses apologizing has not apologized. An imagined conversation might change their anticipated feelings, or not; it does not create a new shared history for the person who has not heard it. Similarly, a child's play can create genuine new capacities and interests without being recorded as a successful adult project. A simulated history must preserve these differences, including their downstream asymmetry of evidence.

An actor may also learn a way of organizing their own attention through social practice. A teacher's repeated demand to “check your work” can become a useful self-monitoring method, an anxious ritual, an empty repetition, a basis for new creative reflection or a source of inhibited initiative. The general transformation is not determined by the phrase. It depends on how the actor used the practice, what was materially possible, which other acquired methods were available and what consequences followed. This is the genesis of a psychological method, not merely a change in the importance of existing content.

### 3. Analogy is a historical wager, not a global semantic equivalence

When Nadia later treats one situation as relevantly like another, the resemblance must be a learned practical achievement. An earlier person might have supplied help with a particular task; a new person offers a superficially similar gesture. Nadia may anticipate comparable relief, distrust the parallel, treat the contexts as unrelated or make an inaccurate transfer. A universal label “helper” cannot replace the history through which one person acquired a way of anticipating aid. Nor does the analyst's recognition of functional similarity prove that the actor explicitly reasoned by analogy.

The inherited functional-correspondence work correctly distinguishes an earlier actor-owned prospective trial, a later actual enactment and the person-specific sign received from it. The first usable analogy is local and defeasible. A tool used successfully in one setting may fail when a new colleague controls its access; a child's cooperative method may be misapplied in a hostile institution. A metaphor that helps someone find an unfamiliar possibility can also trap them in a false one. Subsequent repetition, corrected expectation, social feedback and practice may transform the operation, but none is inevitable.

A newly connected pair of organizations can work in several irreducible ways. One practice may change how another is recruited; two may jointly create an operation neither could previously support; they may conflict and make action difficult; one may be used to suppress the other; they may not interact at all; their relation may remain inaccessible to the observer. We should not reify A×B into a mandatory psychic fusion law. The historical question is always whether a *new effective relation* has been established, what could demonstrate its operation, and which contexts it would fail in.

### 4. The material object is not a universal sign

An instrument, book, door, meal, uniform, wage, coin, gift, photograph or ritual has real socially produced properties before anyone assigns it personal meaning. It can be exchanged, damaged, inherited, withheld, worked upon, displayed or controlled. Its value is carried by material practices and social relations, not only by an actor's private semantic tag. The same object may support a parent's memory, a child's practical experiment, a worker's income, an institution's authority and a stranger's curiosity. These meanings do not have to converge into a single symbolic representation for the object to remain one worldly thing.

Suppose a broken music box is repaired. Nadia initially helps because somebody expects it. During the work a new sound occurs by accident. She notices it, attempts to reproduce it and later relates it to a sound encountered in another setting. The activity may open a private curiosity, a social opportunity, an aversion or nothing further. Another person may value the object economically and care nothing for its sound. A real event can therefore produce the material of a new desire *after* the action; the new end could not have motivated that earlier action in its later form. A model that writes “she secretly always loved music” to explain the discovery has replaced genesis with retrospective destiny.

Objects also carry constraints. A gift cannot be reciprocated if the recipient lacks the means; a tool may be dangerous; a religious text may authorize contradictory acts in different institutions; a favorite space may become inaccessible when property changes hands. The model must be able to alter the object and its effective uses without instantly updating everyone's mind, and to alter an actor's interpretation while leaving the physical object unchanged. It must not treat the independent world as an extension of a personality-tag database.

### 5. Formation is explanatory without becoming predictive omniscience

Across histories, we can identify families of motive formation: bodily need finds an object through participation; an instrumental action acquires its own interest; repeated practice forms a new competence and concern; another person's activity makes an unfamiliar end thinkable; material impossibility intensifies, inhibits or diverts a project; a later self-reinterpretation changes the present sense of a past activity. These families are not one fully validated deterministic transition algorithm. Nor are they the whole of possible development in every culture.

Our scientific obligation is discrimination. We should ask what evidence would show a newly formed operation rather than a renamed desire; whether the person could enact it outside the first context; whether a dissimilar history would fail to produce it; whether it can survive competing obligations; whether it has material means; whether an independently acting Other could change its consequences. A constructed fictional adult may have multiple defensible motive histories conditioned by survey evidence; none is the discovered private truth of the real respondent. Nadia's prospective life is not sampled from an adult destination: new capacities and commitments must actually become possible and historically operative.

**Counterhistory: the double resemblance.** Two adults both receive care from different people, then later encounter an apparently similar helper. One has learned practical trust through reliable participation; the other has learned to distrust conditional favors. A third understands the resemblance and deliberately refuses to act; a fourth never notices it. If our engine simply recognizes “care” and emits reciprocal warmth, it erases the different organization that the same social category conceals.

**Counterhistory: the new end that does not form.** Nadia finds a beautiful piece of music, listens intensely once, and returns to a very different practice. The episode remains meaningful, perhaps even memorable, without becoming a motive or skill. A week later an insignificant tool use creates an unexpected practical enthusiasm. The theory must permit this contrast without invoking a secret destiny to explain the more durable end.

**Counterhistory: the possibility without exercise.** Someone imagines a new life, acquires a plan and understands every practical step, but remains constrained by care obligations, legal status or illness. The imagined future can be psychologically real, and its impossibility can be consequential, while the proposed action never happens. No simulated “success” may enter the world history on the strength of that inward certainty.

## V. The presently living iceberg: simultaneous activities, uneven access and situated expression

### 1. What operates at once is not one contest for the highest score

An acquired psyche is not a drawer from which the world retrieves the currently best-fitting trait. The person may be caring for another while monitoring fatigue, performing a practiced task, anticipating rent, privately disagreeing with a friend, enjoying a sensory rhythm and guarding against an anticipated humiliation. These are not always equally conscious, equally forceful or equally available for verbal report. More importantly, they do not belong to one quantitative dimension. Caring can supply the purpose of one activity while fatigue conditions the bodily means; fear can reorganize attention while a habit carries a task; a material deadline can force an unwanted action despite affection for someone asking for help.

One psychological current may facilitate another without becoming its motive. A remembered insult may change how a harmless remark is heard; concern about a child may make a workplace interruption intolerable; trust can make a previously avoided social context bearable. The same currents may fail to meet. A person might perform a routine act with great competence while another emotionally important conflict remains elsewhere in their life. What counts as an important cause therefore depends on the response being explained: decision, inhibition, timing, affective coloring, bodily orientation, utterance, error, self-account and later afterlife can have partly distinct genealogies.

The inherited PFF vision correctly distinguishes standing organization from momentary activation and observable expression, and the later SOROK/CAN/ADP work preserves independent streams, person–event coupling, relationship-specific mediation and actor-owned temporal traces. But a diagram of available streams does not itself explain composition. A system that simply sums a few scalar tensions and chooses the maximal drive would erase the difference between practical purpose and current feeling, and could not explain why the same person apologizes while still resentful, or refuses while still loving. The actual pressure-flow problem is to make the actor's historically acquired operations function together, against one another, or independently under the concrete constraints of this moment.

### 2. Attention is active, finite, historically trainable and sometimes misleading

An event's occurrence does not imply perception, perception does not imply focal attention, and attention does not imply the formation of a personal meaning. People learn how to notice through activity: a carpenter hears something in a tool's vibration that a visitor misses; a caregiver notices a child's unusual stillness; a newly anxious person becomes vigilant to certain gestures; a musician hears a relationship among notes; a tired worker may miss a familiar warning. Learned salience is not a fixed filter that makes all untrained phenomena literally invisible. Sudden bodily sensation, ambient physical dominance, another person's unexpected act and new practice can break into attention and alter the repertoire.

This acquired selectivity can protect or imprison. The person may recognize a clue while misunderstanding its source; a formerly useful anticipation can produce false alarms. More knowledge can sharpen discrimination, but can also narrow a field around an institutionally learned category. An apparent absence of reaction is compatible with many realities: no perception, no significance, habituation, inhibition, intentional concealment, distraction or simply no change worth reporting. A theory that treats silence as stored trauma, or every noticed object as a new meaning, supplies fabricated psychological depth.

Attention also has self-initiated forms. Solitude can be active rather than empty: waiting, noticing, rehearsing, wishing, dreading, seeking a lost thought, attending to a body, imagining another's perspective. The subject may move into an unfamiliar activity by following an internally available possibility, not because an NPC or visitor created a quest. But the source of this initiative is historical—acquired methods, present body, available time, prior relations and recognized possibilities. To call it “spontaneous” must not mean “the model may instantiate arbitrary new facts.”

### 3. Feeling is a relation in life, not a truth meter

The body may register agitation, warmth, exhaustion, pleasure or discomfort; the person may feel these without knowing what they mean; may interpret them through learned concepts; may dislike the feeling itself; may express it unintentionally; may act from a different concern altogether. Emotion is neither a reward for successful action nor a transparent readout of the deepest motive. A meaning-forming commitment may yield grief or pain. Immediate stimulation may be intense without deciding what matters in the longer life. The person can desire an impossible end, enjoy a practice they intend to abandon, act lovingly while feeling irritable, or feel calm while objectively threatened.

At least five questions matter and must not be answered by one categorical emotion tag: what bodily change occurs, what is experienced, what the actor currently believes about the experience, how the feeling conditions activity and expression, and whether anything later becomes acquired through it. They can diverge. A teenager may feel sudden pleasure at being recognized, judge that pleasure shameful, suppress a smile, privately revisit the event and eventually form an association. Another teenager may display the same smile out of politeness and never think of the encounter again. Shared facial movement does not license the same psychodynamic explanation.

Pleasure, play, attraction, sexual desire, humor, religious devotion, resentment, grief and ordinary boredom require different historically formed objects and practices. The theory cannot use “affect” as a label that turns their differences into one biochemical signal. Erotic life is bodily, learned, socially regulated and relational, but its object, boundaries, actor consent, social meanings and personal history vary. It is not a compulsory outcome of intimacy. Humor can loosen a practice, defend a status hierarchy, signal belonging or conceal embarrassment. Religious experience can organize care, fear, authority, solidarity, ritual competence or practical freedom; no metaphysical verdict about God is needed for a material account of what the experience does in a specific life. These possibilities are not a diagnosis of anyone who displays the behavior.

### 4. Inhibition, conflict, contradictory self-account and no resolution

A person can sincerely believe two things that their practice holds apart. They may want to resist an injustice and dread losing a job; desire a relationship while anticipating humiliation; consider a duty sacred and privately resent it. A conflict can become explicit deliberation, remain an unspoken pattern of avoidance, induce bodily disruption, yield a narrow compromise, generate an entirely new plan or simply continue unresolved. The theory must not presume that dialectical contradiction inherently grants creativity or emancipation.

Sometimes inhibition is practical rather than an intrapsychic repression. A speaker can be silent because time is short, a superior is present, another person is speaking, or a situation affords no safe response. Sometimes a learned relation makes a thought difficult to consider or an emotion difficult to show. Sometimes the person has made a reasoned decision not to disclose. These phenomena may look alike from outside but have different genealogies and ethical implications. A fictional interpreter must not infer an inaccessible cause from the public absence alone.

A person may acquire an internalized condemning voice from social authority while also knowing the institution is wrong. That voice is not a free-floating psychic oppressor: it persists through previously lived practices, social expectations, material sanctions, relationships and the possible future costs of disobedience. A later self-critique can make its origin intelligible without immediately abolishing its practical operation. A person may also remain entirely capable of acting under a norm they no longer endorse. The same acquired history can therefore be rationally rejected and practically preserved.

### 5. From several currents to a single outward episode

The actor must do something or fail to do it under concrete conditions. Consider Nadia asked by a close friend to spend the evening together while she has a difficult shift tomorrow, wants companionship, anticipates an unpaid bill and remains unsettled by an earlier argument. The result cannot be generated by proclaiming that friendship scored 0.6 and exhaustion 0.7. The inquiry is which ongoing activity and object organizes her effort, which anticipated practical costs constrain the action, which relational expectations color the request, what forms of refusal or negotiation she has acquired, what the friend can actually do, and which parts of this organization she can or chooses to express.

She may decline gently, accept and later regret it, propose a shorter meeting, evade the question, change her mind, or be interrupted by an independent event. The same outward refusal can arise from several very different histories. A hesitation can be bodily fatigue, uncertainty about money, anger, a socially learned habit of avoiding direct refusal, or merely an awkward pause. Only a source-qualified historical organization can make one explanation defensible within the fictional life. The renderer must not write the hidden history backward from the most dramatic line of dialogue.

Even when a historically constituted propensity supports expression, the exact utterance is an event, not a direct dump of the psyche. The person can try to speak kindly and sound impatient; try to conceal relief and show it in a body movement; deliberately tell a half-truth; hear their own words and become surprised at what they have revealed. Their voice, gaze, posture, breath, prosody, learned politeness, gestures and habits of silence are acquired expressive resources with material and cultural variation. They can disclose something without trying; they can also conceal something successfully. Neither the visitor's wish to read a clue nor the platform's need for an evocative description authorizes inventing one.

### 6. Hearing oneself is a new event, not an edit to the preceding event

Before speech there may be an intended sentence, several competing candidates, an impulse, a socially rehearsed phrase, or a practical avoidance. The selected actual utterance must be settled as a world event. Only then can the person react to hearing it: embarrassment, surprise, relief, recognition, defensive rationalization or no special notice. That feedback is part of the historical circuit; it cannot be credited before a word was spoken. The same applies to gesture or action. A person can discover how they actually acted and subsequently reconsider their self-understanding, but cannot retroactively perform a different act by deciding the original one feels false.

Memory is involved but does not collapse into the feedback. The organism may carry a procedural orientation or affective afterlife without remembering its first acquisition. An actor can recognize a familiar feeling while wrongly dating its origin; can fail to notice that an old situation is being recruited; can correctly remember what was said yet misjudge its meaning. A standing relation's present operation, explicit episodic recall, accessible narrative self-knowledge and a durable revision of future practices are distinct. The theory must preserve all four even when the narrative surface offers only one.

**Counterhistory: one sentence, four psychologies.** Nadia says, “I can't tonight.” In one life this is straightforward fatigue and competent boundary setting. In another it is an anxious avoidance of a feared conversation. In a third it is genuine anger expressed through a polite convention. In a fourth she has promised to care for someone else and has no conflicted feeling at all. The hearer may read all four as rejection. Only actor-specific history and actual consequences can distinguish them. The system may leave the cause underdetermined if its own prior fictional construction does not warrant an answer.

**Counterhistory: a false leak.** An observer sees Nadia glance toward the door after a difficult remark and decides she is afraid. The glance may instead have answered a sound outside or a scheduled obligation. If the system turns the observer's theory into the actor's actual hidden emotion, it has granted the visitor authorship of the person. If Nadia really is afraid but controls her expression, a renderer that compulsively reveals fear as trembling has made opacity impossible. Both are failures.

## VI. The Other is an independent center of life, not a relational attribute

### 1. Shared events do not create a shared psyche

When two people meet, they share only what actually happens between them, not the whole of what each person has brought to the meeting. One person may remember an unpaid debt, have a bodily need, fear an institutional sanction, anticipate an absent partner's return or seek a specific favor. The other may be engaged in a different project, unaware of those conditions and perfectly sincere in their separate interpretation. A conversation is not an act of merging their histories. It can nevertheless change each life through performed acts, heard words, available help, material harm, practical learning and new anticipation.

This produces a polycentric historical field. There is an objective occurrence, several actor-owned experiences, a set of available or blocked communications, and consequences that can propagate unevenly. A person may be harmed by an event they never witness. A parent can make a sacrifice whose cost is invisible to the child; the child may become dependent on the resulting care without understanding its origin. One friend may regard a shared evening as transformative while the other remembers it only as pleasant. Their unequal histories do not negate the reality of the relation.

The focal actor must therefore not be allowed to create the other person's motives through a sentence such as “she trusts me now.” Nor can the visitor's interpretation of a bodily sign become an objective revelation. Each person has their own capacities, commitments, unfinished business, relations outside the focal dyad, resource constraints and partial knowledge. A real relationship arises through actual interaction among these, not through a common variable called “relationship progress.”

### 2. A relationship has several nonidentical realities

First, two people may have a history of actual interactions. Second, each has acquired person-specific expectations and anticipations about the other. Third, objective means of reachability may exist or fail to exist: a phone, an address, a shared workplace, a friend, a legal permission, a time window. Fourth, each can interpret the history, sometimes incorrectly. Fifth, there are independent material consequences—the transfer of money, care, exclusion, labor, injury, shelter or knowledge. Sixth, each may have a different future orientation toward the relation, including desire, dread, indifference or practical dependence.

These do not necessarily rise and fall together. Frequent contact can produce familiarity without love; deep affection can survive prolonged absence; intimate knowledge can coexist with restricted access; someone can be physically reachable while refusing contact; an expected visitor can no longer arrive after a move or change of work. The theory cannot reduce topology to intimacy or grant the visitor the right to know because they have discovered an address. Reachability requires concrete means, controllers, lawful scope, time conditions, dependencies and possible revocation. It is an objective relation that can be meaningful or meaningless to the actor.

Relational time also matters. A parent may shape a child's bodily routines long before the child can name the relationship; a close colleague may change someone else's practice through repeated work and never become a confidant; a former lover may remain psychologically operative in solitude without being present or contactable. To call the psyche “relational” must not mean everything valuable happens in dialogue. A person who lives alone can still be structured by obligations, past institutions, inherited norms, hoped-for encounters and practical solitude.

### 3. An incidental Other becomes consequential through history, not generation prestige

A stranger can remain incidental. A neighbor may recur in minor exchanges and gradually become a reliable source of information, a friend, an antagonist, or someone whose work alters Nadia's material life. More detailed representation is justified where performed history warrants it, not because a scene's dramatic value suddenly calls for a full biography. When a person becomes more causally consequential, the prior history must constrain what can later be claimed. Previous utterances, knowledge, workplaces, commitments, clocks, material locations, injuries and known absences cannot be overwritten by a convenient childhood invention.

Unknown details are not necessarily empty. A provisional childhood may be historically plausible within the world and cultural environment but cannot contradict what has been settled. An unrepresented earlier cause is different from an unknown currently observable fact, and both differ from a documented impossibility. The model may refine bounded historical slack when later depth is necessary, but it may not use slack to retroactively supply any event needed to justify a preferred reaction. This is equally true for Nadia's parents, neighbors and absent relations, though the initial evidentiary entrance differs for a constructed parent and a continuously simulated child.

The actor is not the only author of a relation's outcome. A generous offer can be declined; a promise can fail because transport collapses; an attempted reconciliation can be refused; coercion can produce unilateral harm without the victim's consent. Voluntary joint acts require independent cooperation, while unilateral actions may affect unwilling people. A world in which every intended kindness succeeds and every invitation becomes a connection is not a world with other persons. A world in which every other person exists to oppose the protagonist is equally false.

### 4. Care and obligation join histories without making their meanings identical

Suppose Nadia's mother takes an extra shift to pay for a school instrument. Her action can be an actual sacrifice, labor in an exploitative arrangement, a choice expressive of commitment, an occasion for resentment and an enabling condition of the child's new capacity. Nadia may enjoy the instrument, regard it as a burden, misrecognize the mother's motive, later learn the cost, or never know. The mother's psychological life is not whatever the child eventually thinks about it. The child's developing interest is not the mother's will reproduced in smaller form. The instrument remains a materially produced object which others may repair, inherit or sell.

Care can thus genuinely sustain two lives while producing unequal effects. One person learns the labor of soothing; another learns that relief tends to arrive. The same practice can become a source of trust, dependence, guilt, competence or no enduring special meaning. A third actor might supply the unpaid household work that makes the soothing possible; an employer might determine its timing. Their actual causal contributions must not disappear because the visible pair is emotionally compelling. Neither relational love nor class power monopolizes the explanation.

### 5. The ethical horizon is neither guaranteed reciprocity nor compulsory solidarity

The possibility of knowing another person is real; one person can become deeply familiar with another's expressive habits, material needs and history. Recognition can be accurate, pleasurable and transformative. But recognition need not be perfect, and gaining access is not an entitlement to the Other's full interior. Misunderstanding may persist after years. The other person may be uninterested in the relationship, be injured by it, change in a direction the focal actor dislikes or simply move into a different life.

This is not a cynical denial of companionship. It makes companionship more substantial: two histories can sustain expectations, shared pleasures, mutual competence and care without becoming one consciousness. Each participant can receive what the other really does, and experience it differently. A friendship can alter both while preserving irreducible difference. It can also produce betrayal, failure, stagnation, recovery or separation. No score that mechanically rewards intimacy, and no predetermined liberation narrative, can substitute for the historically formed practice.

**Counterhistory: two unequal afterlives.** Nadia and a friend repair a broken stair. Nadia experiences practical competence and relief. The friend remembers being ignored and decides not to cooperate again. The stair is objectively safer; the relation may be worse; the repair skill may become more available to one person and not the other. An engine that updates shared “teamwork” and increases mutual trust has falsely unified the event.

**Counterhistory: reachable but unwanted.** An old friend knows Nadia's address, has called often and can travel to her house. She does not want to speak today. The existence of a route is not permission to enter or proof of an available relationship. Refusal can be competent boundary setting without any hidden hostility; the visitor might nevertheless feel wounded. The two feelings are not a single moral judgment.

**Counterhistory: shared affection under different constraints.** Two siblings care deeply for their father. One can afford to travel to him; the other cannot leave work and lacks money. The first experiences physical closeness and resentment; the second is distant and ashamed; the father interprets absence as indifference. Affection, actual presence, objective resources, interpretation and lasting rupture can all diverge. A theory that makes each person equally responsible for the spatial outcome has abolished power; a theory that predicts their precise feelings from income has abolished particular history.
